    Section 3.1 First-Order ODEs

    An ordinary differential equation (ODE) relates an unknown function to its derivatives. This section develops the theory and solution methods for first-order ODEs, the most fundamental class. We begin by defining ODEs and clarifying what a solution is. We then study how to extract qualitative information from an ODE without solving it, using slope fields and sign arguments. Finally, we treat the two main solvable classes in full: separable ODEs (solved by integrating both sides after separating variables) and linear first-order ODEs (solved by the variation-of-constants formula). Throughout, engineering applications — population models, RC circuits — illustrate each concept.
    🔗

    Subsection 3.1.1 Ordinary Differential Equations and Their Solutions

    Many processes in engineering and the natural sciences are most naturally described not by an explicit formula for an unknown quantity, but by a relationship between that quantity and its rates of change. Such a relationship is called a differential equation. In this section we define ordinary differential equations (ODEs), clarify what it means to solve one, and introduce the distinction between general and particular solutions.
    🔗

    Example 3.1.1. Population Growth: A Motivating Example.

    We model the growth of a population (for example, a colony of bacteria).
    🔗
    (a) Simple proportional growth. In the simplest case, the growth rate is proportional to the current population size. Let \(p(t)\) denote the population at time \(t\text{.}\) This assumption translates directly into the equation
    \begin{equation*} p'(t) = k \, p(t), \end{equation*}
    where \(k\) is the proportionality constant. The unknown is the function \(p\text{;}\) the equation involves both \(p\) and its derivative \(p'\text{.}\) This is a first-order ordinary differential equation, and its solutions describe exponential growth: \(p(t) = p_0 \, e^{kt}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) Logistic growth. A more realistic model assumes that the population approaches a maximum value \(p_{\max} = 1\) (normalised to \(100\%\)). The growth rate is proportional both to the current population and to the remaining capacity \(1 - p(t)\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} p'(t) = k \, p(t) \, (1 - p(t)). \end{equation*}
    This is again a first-order ODE, but its solutions exhibit an S-shaped (sigmoidal) curve: slow initial growth, rapid growth near \(p = 0.5\text{,}\) and levelling off as \(p \to 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 3.1.4. Ordinary Differential Equation.

    An ordinary differential equation (ODE) of order \(n\) is an equation in an unknown function \(y = y(t)\) of a single real variable \(t\) in which the function and its derivatives up to order \(n\) appear:
    \begin{equation*} y^{(n)}(t) = f\!\bigl(t,\, y(t),\, y'(t),\, \ldots,\, y^{(n-1)}(t)\bigr). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 3.1.5. Why “Ordinary”?

    This definition describes an explicit ODE: the highest derivative \(y^{(n)}\) is isolated on the left. All ODEs in this section have this form.
    🔗
    The qualifier “ordinary” signals that the unknown function depends on a single variable. By contrast, a partial differential equation (PDE) involves an unknown function of several variables (e.g.\ temperature \(u(x, t)\)), with partial derivatives in each variable. The heat equation and the wave equation are classical PDEs and belong to more advanced courses.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.1.6. Examples of ODEs and Their Orders.

    • \(p'(t) = k \, p(t) \, (1 - p(t))\) is an ODE of order 1.
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(y''(t) = 2 y'(t) - 7 y(t) + 14\) is an ODE of order 2.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.1.7. Free Fall: Solving by Integration.

    The position \(y(t)\) of a mass in free fall with constant downward acceleration \(g\) satisfies Newton’s second law:
    \begin{equation*} y''(t) = g. \end{equation*}
    This is a second-order ODE. Because the right-hand side does not involve \(y\) or \(y'\text{,}\) we can solve it by integrating twice:
    \begin{align*} \int y''(t)\, dt \amp= \int g\, dt \quad \Rightarrow \quad y'(t) = g t + C_1,\\ \int y'(t)\, dt \amp= \int (g t + C_1)\, dt \quad \Rightarrow \quad y(t) = \tfrac{1}{2} g t^2 + C_1 t + C_2. \end{align*}
    The general solution \(y(t) = \frac{1}{2} g t^2 + C_1 t + C_2\) contains two free constants \(C_1, C_2 \in \R\text{,}\) reflecting the fact that the ODE has order 2.
    🔗
    To determine \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) one supplies additional conditions:
    • Initial value problem (IVP): specify \(y(t_0)\) and \(y'(t_0)\text{.}\) For example, \(y(0) = 0\) gives \(C_2 = 0\) and \(y'(0) = 0\) gives \(C_1 = 0\text{,}\) yielding \(y(t) = \frac{1}{2} g t^2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Boundary value problem (BVP): specify \(y(t_0)\) and \(y(t_1)\) at two different times.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 3.1.8. Solution of an ODE; General and Particular Solutions.

    A solution of the ODE \(y^{(n)}(t) = f\bigl(t, y(t), \ldots, y^{(n-1)}(t)\bigr)\) is an \(n\)-times differentiable function \(y = y(t)\) that satisfies the equation for all \(t\) in its domain.
    • For the standard solvable families studied in this course, the general solution of an order-\(n\) ODE contains \(n\) arbitrary constants (constants of integration).
      🔗
      🔗
    • A particular solution contains no free parameters; it is obtained by imposing \(n\) initial or boundary conditions.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.1.9. Verifying a Solution.

    Which of the following functions satisfies \(y'' + y = -\sin x\text{?}\)
    🔗
    (A) \(f_A(x) = \sin x\) \(\quad\) (B) \(f_B(x) = \tfrac{1}{2} x \sin x\) \(\quad\) (C) \(f_C(x) = \cos x\) \(\quad\) (D) \(f_D(x) = \tfrac{1}{2} x \cos x\)
    🔗
    Answer. We verify option (D): let \(y = \tfrac{1}{2} x \cos x\text{.}\)
    \begin{align*} y' \amp= \tfrac{1}{2}\cos x - \tfrac{1}{2} x \sin x,\\ y'' \amp= -\tfrac{1}{2}\sin x - \tfrac{1}{2}\sin x - \tfrac{1}{2} x \cos x = -\sin x - \tfrac{1}{2} x \cos x. \end{align*}
    Hence
    \begin{equation*} y'' + y = -\sin x - \tfrac{1}{2} x \cos x + \tfrac{1}{2} x \cos x = -\sin x. \quad \checkmark \end{equation*}
    Options (A), (B), (C) can be verified to fail by a similar computation.
    🔗
    🔗

    Exercises Check Your Understanding

    1. Recognise the Order.
    What is the order of the differential equation
    \begin{equation*} y'''+2y=t? \end{equation*}
    🔗
    • First order
    • Incorrect. The highest derivative is not \(y'\text{.}\)
    • Second order
    • Incorrect. The equation contains a third derivative.
    • Third order
    • Correct. The highest derivative appearing is \(y'''\text{.}\)
    • It has no order because the right-hand side is \(t\text{.}\)
    • Incorrect. The order depends only on the highest derivative of the unknown function.
    🔗
    2. Verify a Candidate Solution.
    Verify that
    \begin{equation*} y(t)=e^{2t} \end{equation*}
    solves the ODE
    \begin{equation*} y'=2y. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    Differentiate:
    \begin{equation*} y'(t)=2e^{2t}. \end{equation*}
    On the other hand,
    \begin{equation*} 2y(t)=2e^{2t}. \end{equation*}
    Hence \(y'(t)=2y(t)\) for all \(t\text{,}\) so the function is indeed a solution.

    Subsection 3.1.2 Qualitative Analysis and Slope Fields

    Before solving an ODE analytically, it is valuable to understand the qualitative behaviour of its solutions: where are they increasing or decreasing? Are they bounded? Do they level off? Visualising the slope field of an ODE makes these questions accessible without integration and reveals structure that guides the analytic work.
    🔗

    Example 3.1.10. Excluding Functions as Solutions by Graph Shape.

    Consider the ODE
    \begin{equation*} y'(t) = e^{t}(y - 1)^{2}. \end{equation*}
    We explain why certain graph shapes are incompatible with being a solution.
    🔗
    • A function with a local maximum has a region of negative slope. But for any solution of this ODE we have \(y'(t) = e^t(y-1)^2 \geq 0\) everywhere (since \(e^t > 0\) and \((y-1)^2 \geq 0\)). A negative slope is impossible.
      🔗
      🔗
    • An increasing function that crosses \(y = 1\text{.}\) At any point where \(y(t_0) = 1\text{,}\) the ODE gives \(y'(t_0) = e^{t_0}(1-1)^2 = 0\text{.}\) A solution must have slope zero whenever it reaches \(y = 1\text{;}\) a transversal increasing crossing is impossible.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    These observations illustrate a powerful principle: the ODE itself tells us the slope at every point on the solution curve, giving direct constraints on the graph without integration.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.1.12. Identifying an ODE from the Shape of Its Solution.

    Suppose we observe a bell-shaped solution curve \(y = y(x)\) with a single peak at \(x = 0\text{.}\) Which of the following ODEs is consistent with this behaviour?
    🔗
    (A) \(y' = 1 + xy\) \(\quad\) (B) \(y' = -2xy\) \(\quad\) (C) \(y' = 1 - 2xy\)
    🔗
    Answer. At the peak, the slope must be zero: \(y'(0) = 0\text{.}\)
    • (A): \(y'(0) = 1 + 0 \cdot y(0) = 1 \neq 0\text{.}\) Ruled out.
      🔗
      🔗
    • (B): \(y'(0) = -2 \cdot 0 \cdot y(0) = 0\text{.}\) Consistent with a peak at \(x = 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • (C): \(y'(0) = 1 - 0 = 1 \neq 0\text{.}\) Ruled out.
      🔗
      🔗
    Only equation (B) \(y' = -2xy\) is consistent. Indeed, \(y(x) = e^{-x^2}\) solves (B) and has the described bell shape.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 3.1.14. Slope Fields.

    For a first-order ODE \(y'(t) = f(t, y(t))\text{,}\) the slope field (or direction field) is drawn by placing a short line segment at each point \((t, y)\) with slope \(f(t, y)\text{.}\) Any solution curve through an initial point \((t_0, y_0)\) is tangent to the slope field everywhere it passes — so sketching a solution amounts to “flowing along” the arrows from \((t_0, y_0)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Observe that the two solution curves never cross. This is not a coincidence: for a first-order ODE with a sufficiently regular right-hand side, the initial value uniquely determines the solution (Picard–Lindelöf theorem, proved in more advanced courses).
    🔗
    🔗

    Exercises Check Your Understanding

    1. Equilibrium Solutions.
    Consider the ODE
    \begin{equation*} y'=y(1-y). \end{equation*}
    Which constant solutions are equilibrium solutions?
    🔗
    • Only \(y\equiv 0\)
    • Incorrect. There is another constant value that also makes the right-hand side zero.
    • Only \(y\equiv 1\)
    • Incorrect. Both factors of \(y(1-y)\) matter.
    • \(y\equiv 0\) and \(y\equiv 1\)
    • Correct. Both constant values make \(y(1-y)=0\text{.}\)
    • There are no equilibrium solutions.
    • Incorrect. Equilibria occur when the right-hand side vanishes.
    🔗
    2. Read Off a Slope.
    For the ODE
    \begin{equation*} y'=t-y, \end{equation*}
    what is the slope of a solution curve at the point \((t,y)=(0,2)\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Substitute \(t=0\) and \(y=2\) into the right-hand side:
    \begin{equation*} y'=0-2=-2. \end{equation*}
    So the solution curve has slope \(-2\) at that point.

    Subsection 3.1.3 Separable ODEs

    The simplest first-order ODEs that can be solved by explicit integration are the separable ones: the right-hand side factors into a function of \(y\) alone times a function of \(t\) alone. This structure lets us move all \(y\)-dependent terms to one side and all \(t\)-dependent terms to the other before integrating.
    🔗

    Definition 3.1.16. Separable ODE.

    A first-order ODE is separable if it can be written in the form
    \begin{equation*} y'(t) = g(y) \cdot h(t) \end{equation*}
    for functions \(g\) (depending only on \(y\)) and \(h\) (depending only on \(t\)).
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.1.17. Identifying Separable ODEs.

    1. \(t\, y'(t) = \dfrac{y(t)}{t^2}\text{,}\) equivalently \(y' = y \cdot t^{-3}\text{.}\) Separable with \(g(y) = y\) and \(h(t) = t^{-3}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(t^2 y'(t) = \sin(y(t) + t)\text{.}\) Not separable: \(y\) and \(t\) appear together inside \(\sin\) and cannot be factored apart.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(y'(t) = e^{t + y(t)}\text{.}\) Separable: \(e^{t+y} = e^t \cdot e^y\text{,}\) so \(g(y) = e^y\) and \(h(t) = e^t\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(\sqrt{t}\, y'(t) = (y(t))^2 + t\text{.}\) Not separable: the sum \(y^2 + t\) cannot be written as a product of a \(y\)-function and a \(t\)-function.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 3.1.18. Method of Separation of Variables.

    To solve the separable ODE \(y'(t) = g(y) \cdot h(t)\text{:}\)
    🔗
    1. Separate variables (assuming \(g(y) \neq 0\)):
      \begin{equation*} \frac{y'(t)}{g(y(t))} = h(t). \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Integrate both sides using the substitution \(u = y(t)\text{,}\) \(du = y'(t)\, dt\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} \int \frac{1}{g(u)}\, du = \int h(t)\, dt + C, \qquad C \in \R. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Solve for \(y(t)\) from the equation \(\displaystyle\int \frac{1}{g(u)}\, du = \int h(t)\, dt + C\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Check constant solutions. Determine all constants \(c\) for which \(g(c) = 0\) (these were excluded in Step 1) and add the corresponding constant solutions \(y(t) \equiv c\) if they are not already included in the general solution.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.1.19. Solving the Exponential Growth ODE.

    Solve \(p'(t) = k\, p(t)\) for \(k \in \R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 1: Separate variables (for \(p(t) \neq 0\)):
    \begin{equation*} \frac{p'(t)}{p(t)} = k. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Step 2: Integrate both sides. With the substitution \(u = p(t)\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} \int \frac{1}{u}\, du \amp= \int k\, dt + C\\ \ln|p(t)| \amp= kt + C. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Step 3: Solve for \(p(t)\text{.}\) Applying the exponential function:
    \begin{equation*} |p(t)| = e^{kt + C} = e^C \cdot e^{kt}. \end{equation*}
    Writing \(A = \pm e^C\text{,}\) we can absorb both the sign and the positive factor \(e^C\) into a single nonzero constant and obtain
    \begin{equation*} p(t) = A \, e^{kt}, \qquad A \in \R \setminus \{0\}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Step 4: Check constant solutions. The constant function \(p(t) \equiv 0\) satisfies \(0' = 0 = k \cdot 0\text{,}\) so it is also a solution. Including it (corresponding to \(A = 0\)) gives the general solution:
    \begin{equation*} p(t) = C\, e^{kt}, \qquad C \in \R. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.1.20. Solving the Logistic Growth ODE.

    Consider the logistic model
    \begin{equation*} p'(t) = k\, p(t)\!\left(1 - \frac{p(t)}{p_{\max}}\right), \qquad k > 0,\quad 0 \leq p(t) \leq p_{\max}. \end{equation*}
    This is separable with \(g(p) = p\!\left(1 - p/p_{\max}\right)\) and \(h(t) = k\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For the special case \(p_{\max} = 1\text{,}\) \(k = 1\text{,}\) a partial-fraction decomposition gives
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{p(1-p)} = \frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{1-p}. \end{equation*}
    Integrating the separated equation:
    \begin{equation*} \ln|p| - \ln|1 - p| = t + C \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \frac{p}{1 - p} = A e^{t}, \quad A = e^C. \end{equation*}
    Solving for \(p\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} p(t) = \frac{A e^{t}}{1 + A e^{t}} = \frac{1}{1 + B e^{-t}}, \qquad B = A^{-1}. \end{equation*}
    This is the logistic (sigmoid) function. As \(t \to +\infty\) it approaches \(1 = p_{\max}\text{;}\) as \(t \to -\infty\) it approaches 0. This confirms the S-shaped behaviour shown in Figure 3.1.3.
    🔗
    The same separation method with general \(k\) and \(p_{\max}\) leads to the engineering form
    \begin{equation*} p(t) = \frac{p_{\max}}{1 + B e^{-kt}}, \end{equation*}
    where \(B\) is determined by the initial value. In addition, the constant functions \(p(t) \equiv 0\) and \(p(t) \equiv p_{\max}\) are equilibrium solutions because they make the right-hand side vanish.
    🔗
    🔗

    Exercises Check Your Understanding

    1. Identify a Separable ODE.
    Which of the following differential equations is separable?
    🔗
    • \(y'=2t\,y\)
    • Correct. It can be written as \(\frac{1}{y}\,dy = 2t\,dt\text{.}\)
    • \(y'+y=t\)\)
    • Incorrect. This is linear, but not already in separated form.
    • \(y''+y=0\)
    • Incorrect. This is not even a first-order ODE.
    • \(y'=t+y\)
    • Incorrect. The variables do not separate into a product of a function of \(t\) and a function of \(y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    2. Solve a Simple Separable ODE.
    Solve the initial value problem
    \begin{equation*} y'=2t\,y, \qquad y(0)=3. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    Separate variables:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{y}\,dy = 2t\,dt. \end{equation*}
    Integrating gives
    \begin{equation*} \ln|y| = t^2 + C. \end{equation*}
    Exponentiating,
    \begin{equation*} y = C_1 e^{t^2}. \end{equation*}
    Using \(y(0)=3\) yields \(C_1=3\text{.}\) Therefore
    \begin{equation*} y(t)=3e^{t^2}. \end{equation*}

    Subsection 3.1.4 Linear First-Order ODEs

    A first-order ODE is linear when \(y\) and \(y'\) appear only to the first power and are never multiplied together or fed into nonlinear functions. Linear ODEs arise throughout engineering: RC circuits, mixing problems, Newton’s law of cooling, and many more. Their key feature is that an explicit general solution formula always exists, derived by a technique called variation of constants.
    🔗

    Definition 3.1.21. Linear First-Order ODE; Homogeneous and Inhomogeneous.

    A first-order ODE is linear if it can be written as
    \begin{equation*} y'(t) + a(t)\, y(t) = b(t), \end{equation*}
    where \(a(t)\) and \(b(t)\) are given functions of \(t\text{.}\)
    • If \(b(t) = 0\text{,}\) the ODE is homogeneous. Every homogeneous linear first-order ODE is also separable.
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(b(t) \neq 0\text{,}\) the ODE is inhomogeneous.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.1.22. Classifying Linear ODEs.

    1. \(y'(t) + e^{4t} y(t) = y(t)\text{,}\) equivalently \(y' + (e^{4t} - 1)y = 0\text{.}\) Linear, homogeneous: \(a(t) = e^{4t} - 1\text{,}\) \(b(t) = 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(y'(t) + \sin(4t \cdot y(t)) = 0\text{.}\) Not linear: \(y\) appears as the argument of \(\sin\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(y'(t) + t\, y(t) = t^4\text{.}\) Linear, inhomogeneous: \(a(t) = t\text{,}\) \(b(t) = t^4\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 3.1.23. General Solution of the Homogeneous Linear First-Order ODE.

    Let \(A(t)\) be an antiderivative of \(a(t)\text{,}\) i.e. \(A'(t) = a(t)\text{.}\) The general solution of the homogeneous ODE \(y'(t) + a(t)\, y(t) = 0\) is
    \begin{equation*} y(t) = C\, e^{-A(t)}, \qquad C \in \R. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    The homogeneous ODE is separable: for \(y \neq 0\text{,}\) \(y'/y = -a(t)\text{.}\) Integrating: \(\ln|y| = -A(t) + \text{const}\text{,}\) so \(|y| = e^{-A(t)+\text{const}}\text{.}\) Absorbing the sign and the constant into \(C\) gives \(y(t) = C e^{-A(t)}\text{,}\) and the zero solution corresponds to \(C = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.1.24. Discharging a Capacitor (RC Circuit).

    An RC series circuit consists of a resistor with resistance \(R\) and a capacitor with capacitance \(C\) connected in a loop. When the capacitor is pre-charged to voltage \(U_0\) at time \(t = 0\) and the circuit is closed with no external source, the capacitor voltage \(U(t)\) satisfies
    \begin{equation*} U'(t) + \frac{1}{RC}\, U(t) = 0. \end{equation*}
    This is a homogeneous linear first-order ODE with the constant coefficient \(a = \frac{1}{RC}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    With \(A(t) = \frac{t}{RC}\text{,}\) the theorem gives
    \begin{equation*} U(t) = C_1\, e^{-t/(RC)}. \end{equation*}
    Applying the initial condition \(U(0) = U_0\) yields \(C_1 = U_0\text{.}\) The particular solution is
    \begin{equation*} U(t) = U_0\, e^{-t/(RC)}, \end{equation*}
    describing exponential decay with time constant \(\tau = RC\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Next, we turn from homogeneous equations to inhomogeneous linear ODEs of first order.
    🔗

    Theorem 3.1.26. Variation of Constants.

    Fix a point \(t_0\) in the interval of interest and set
    \begin{equation*} A(t) = \int_{t_0}^{t} a(s)\,ds. \end{equation*}
    The general solution of the inhomogeneous linear ODE \(y'(t) + a(t)\, y(t) = b(t)\) is
    \begin{equation*} y(t) = e^{-A(t)} \left(C + \int_{t_0}^{t} e^{A(s)} b(s)\,ds\right), \qquad C \in \R. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 3.1.27. Structure of the General Solution.

    The formula above decomposes naturally into two parts:
    \begin{equation*} y(t) = \underbrace{C\, e^{-A(t)}}_{y_h(t)} + \underbrace{e^{-A(t)} \int_{t_0}^{t} e^{A(s)} b(s)\,ds}_{y_p(t)}. \end{equation*}
    • \(y_h(t) = C\, e^{-A(t)}\) is the general solution of the corresponding homogeneous ODE; it contains the free constant \(C\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(y_p(t) = e^{-A(t)} \int_{t_0}^{t} e^{A(s)} b(s)\,ds\) is a particular solution of the inhomogeneous ODE.
      🔗
      🔗
    This additive structure — general homogeneous solution plus a particular inhomogeneous solution — recurs in Section 3-2 for higher-order linear ODEs.
    🔗
    🔗

    Derivation of Variation of Constants.

    The general homogeneous solution is \(y_h = C e^{-A(t)}\text{.}\) We “vary the constant” by replacing \(C\) with an unknown function \(u(t)\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} y(t) = u(t)\, e^{-A(t)}. \end{equation*}
    Differentiating and substituting into \(y' + a(t) y = b(t)\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} y'(t) \amp= u'(t)\, e^{-A(t)} + u(t)\cdot(-a(t))\, e^{-A(t)},\\ y' + a(t) y \amp= u'(t)\, e^{-A(t)} - a(t) u(t) e^{-A(t)} + a(t) u(t) e^{-A(t)} = u'(t)\, e^{-A(t)}. \end{align*}
    The two \(a(t)\)-terms cancel, leaving \(u'(t)\, e^{-A(t)} = b(t)\text{,}\) i.e.
    \begin{equation*} u'(t) = b(t)\, e^{A(t)}. \end{equation*}
    Integrating from \(t_0\) to \(t\) gives
    \begin{equation*} u(t) = C + \int_{t_0}^{t} b(s)\, e^{A(s)}\,ds. \end{equation*}
    Substituting back gives the stated formula.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.1.28. Charging a Capacitor (RC Circuit with Voltage Source).

    Now assume the capacitor is uncharged at \(t = 0\) and a constant voltage source \(U_0\) is switched in. The governing ODE becomes inhomogeneous:
    \begin{equation*} U'(t) + \frac{1}{RC}\, U(t) = \frac{U_0}{RC}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We apply variation of constants with \(t_0=0\text{,}\) \(a(t) = \frac{1}{RC}\text{,}\) \(A(t) = \int_0^t \frac{1}{RC}\,ds = \frac{t}{RC}\text{,}\) \(b(t) = \frac{U_0}{RC}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^t \frac{U_0}{RC}\, e^{s/RC}\,ds = U_0\bigl(e^{t/RC}-1\bigr). \end{equation*}
    Hence
    \begin{equation*} U(t) = e^{-t/RC}\left(C_1 + U_0\bigl(e^{t/RC}-1\bigr)\right) = U_0 + (C_1-U_0)\, e^{-t/RC}. \end{equation*}
    Applying \(U(0) = 0\text{:}\) \(C_1 = 0\text{.}\) The particular solution is
    \begin{equation*} U(t) = U_0\!\left(1 - e^{-t/(RC)}\right), \end{equation*}
    an exponential rise from \(0\) towards the source voltage \(U_0\) with the same time constant \(\tau = RC\text{.}\)
    🔗
    After one time constant \(t = \tau = RC\text{,}\) the voltage has reached \(U(RC) = U_0(1 - e^{-1}) \approx 0.63\, U_0\text{,}\) a useful rule of thumb in circuit design.

    Exercises Check Your Understanding

    1. Identify a Linear First-Order ODE.
    Which equation is a linear first-order ODE?
    🔗
    • \(y'+2t\,y=\sin t\)
    • Correct. It has the form \(y'+a(t)y=b(t)\text{.}\)
    • \(y'+y^2=0\)
    • Incorrect. The term \(y^2\) makes the equation nonlinear.
    • \(yy'+t=0\)
    • Incorrect. The product \(yy'\) is nonlinear.
    • \(y''+y=0\)
    • Incorrect. This equation is second order.
    🔗
    2. Solve a Homogeneous Linear ODE.
    Solve
    \begin{equation*} y'+y=0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    This is a homogeneous linear first-order ODE. Rewriting gives
    \begin{equation*} y'=-y. \end{equation*}
    Separating variables,
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{y}\,dy = -dt. \end{equation*}
    Integration yields
    \begin{equation*} \ln|y|=-t+C. \end{equation*}
    Hence
    \begin{equation*} y(t)=Ce^{-t}, \qquad C\in\R. \end{equation*}
