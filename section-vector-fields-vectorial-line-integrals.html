    Section 4.3 Vector Fields and Vectorial Line Integrals

    This section extends the theory of line integrals to vector fields. Whereas a scalar line integral integrates a scalar function along a curve (weighting by arc length), a vectorial line integral integrates a vector field along a curve by taking the dot product with the curve’s tangent vector at each point. Only the component of the field aligned with the direction of motion contributes to the integral.
    🔗
    We begin by studying vector fields themselves — functions that assign a vector to every point of their domain — and introduce the three fundamental differential operators of vector calculus: the gradient (which produces a vector field from a scalar field), the divergence (which produces a scalar field from a vector field), and the curl (which produces a vector field from a vector field in three dimensions).
    🔗
    We then define the vectorial line integral and establish its central theoretical result: the fundamental theorem for line integrals. For conservative vector fields — those that possess an antiderivative — the integral reduces to a boundary evaluation, just as in the one-variable case. This leads to the notion of path independence and to practical methods for finding antiderivatives of vector fields.
    🔗

    Subsection 4.3.1 Vector Fields

    Until now we have studied scalar fields: functions that assign a number to each point in a domain. A vector field instead assigns a vector to each point. Vector fields arise wherever a physical quantity has both magnitude and direction that varies from place to place: the velocity of a flowing fluid, the force exerted by gravity, or the electric field surrounding a charged particle. Understanding how to describe and manipulate vector fields is the foundation of the rest of this section.
    🔗

    Definition 4.3.1. Vector Field, Continuity, and Differentiability.

    Let \(D \subseteq \R^n\text{.}\) A vector field on \(D\) is a function
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v} \colon D \to \R^n, \qquad \mathbf{x} \mapsto \mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x}) = \begin{pmatrix} v_1(\mathbf{x}) \\ \vdots \\ v_n(\mathbf{x}) \end{pmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    that assigns a vector to each point of \(D\text{.}\) The functions \(v_1, \ldots, v_n\colon D \to \R\) are called the coordinate functions (or components) of \(\mathbf{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The vector field \(\mathbf{v}\) is called continuous (respectively differentiable) if each of its coordinate functions \(v_1, \ldots, v_n\) is continuous (respectively differentiable).
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 4.3.2. Physical Examples of Vector Fields.

    Vector fields appear throughout engineering and physics.
    🔗
    Meteorology. Wind can be modeled as a velocity vector field \(\mathbf{v}\colon \R^2 \to \R^2\) over a geographic region: at each point \((x,y)\) the vector \(\mathbf{v}(x,y)\) gives the wind speed and direction. Weather maps visualize such fields as grids of arrows whose length encodes speed and whose direction encodes the wind direction.
    🔗
    Aerodynamics. The air flow around a wing is a three-dimensional velocity vector field \(\mathbf{v}\colon \R^3 \to \R^3\text{.}\) Engineers compute pressure and lift from the behavior of this field near the surface of the wing.
    🔗
    The figure below shows a simple planar rotation field \(\mathbf{v}(x,y) = \bigl(-y,\, x\bigr)\) as a representative example. At each grid point an arrow is drawn in the direction of \(\mathbf{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 4.3.4. Central Force Field (Newton’s Law of Gravitation).

    A point mass \(M\) located at the origin generates a gravitational force field. According to Newton’s law of gravitation, the force on a test mass at position \(\mathbf{x} \in \R^3 \setminus \{\mathbf{0}\}\) is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}) = -\frac{MG}{\|\mathbf{x}\|^3}\,\mathbf{x}, \end{equation*}
    where \(G\) is the universal gravitational constant. This can be rewritten as
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}) = -\frac{MG}{\|\mathbf{x}\|^2} \cdot \frac{\mathbf{x}}{\|\mathbf{x}\|}, \end{equation*}
    showing that the force has magnitude \(MG/\|\mathbf{x}\|^2\) and points in the direction \(-\mathbf{x}/\|\mathbf{x}\|\text{,}\) i.e.\ toward the origin. This is a central force field: all vectors point toward a single center and their length depends only on the distance from the center.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.3.5. Identifying a Vector Field from Its Properties.

    Which of the following vector fields on \(\R^2\) has the property that at all points with \(x \neq 0\text{,}\) vectors point away from the \(y\)-axis and all vectors along any fixed vertical line have the same length?
    🔗
    • (A) \(\mathbf{v}(x,y) = \begin{pmatrix} x^3 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • (B) \(\mathbf{v}(x,y) = \begin{pmatrix} x^2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • (C) \(\mathbf{v}(x,y) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ x^3 \end{pmatrix}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • (D) \(\mathbf{v}(x,y) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ x^2 \end{pmatrix}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Solution.
    Away from the \(y\)-axis means the first component is positive for \(x > 0\) and negative for \(x < 0\text{.}\) This rules out (B), where \(x^2 \geq 0\) always, and (C), (D), where the horizontal component is zero — the vectors are vertical, not pointing away from the axis.
    🔗
    Same length along a vertical line (i.e.\ constant \(x\)) means the vector length depends only on \(x\text{,}\) not on \(y\text{.}\) For (A), \(\|\mathbf{v}(x,y)\| = |x^3|\text{,}\) which depends only on \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The answer is (A).

    Subsection 4.3.2 Gradient Fields, Divergence, and Curl

    Every smooth scalar field gives rise to a vector field via the gradient. The reverse question — whether a given vector field comes from some scalar field — leads to the concepts of antiderivative and conservative field. Two further differential operators, divergence and curl, capture how a vector field spreads outward from a point or how it swirls around an axis. These three operations — gradient, divergence, and curl — are the core tools of vector calculus.
    🔗

    Definition 4.3.6. Gradient Field, Antiderivative, Scalar Potential, Conservative Field.

    Let \(D \subseteq \R^n\) and let \(f\colon D \to \R\) be a differentiable scalar function. The gradient field of \(f\) is the vector field
    \begin{equation*} \nabla f \colon D \to \R^n, \qquad \mathbf{x} \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}(\mathbf{x}) \\[4pt] \vdots \\[2pt] \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}(\mathbf{x}) \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Let \(\mathbf{F}\colon D \to \R^n\) be a vector field. A differentiable scalar function \(f\colon D \to \R\) satisfying \(\nabla f = \mathbf{F}\) is called an antiderivative (or potential function) of \(\mathbf{F}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    In force-field applications one often uses the negative function \(U = -f\) as a potential energy, so that \(\mathbf{F} = -\nabla U\text{.}\)
    🔗
    A vector field that possesses an antiderivative is called a conservative vector field.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.3.7. The Gravitational Field Is Conservative.

    Show that the central force field \(\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}) = -\dfrac{MG}{\|\mathbf{x}\|^3}\,\mathbf{x}\) on \(\R^3 \setminus \{\mathbf{0}\}\) has the antiderivative \(f(\mathbf{x}) = \dfrac{MG}{\|\mathbf{x}\|}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We compute \(\nabla f\) and check that it equals \(\mathbf{F}\text{.}\) Write \(\|\mathbf{x}\| = \sqrt{x_1^2 + x_2^2 + x_3^2}\text{.}\) Differentiating with respect to \(x_1\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}(\mathbf{x}) \amp= \frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} \bigl(MG\,(x_1^2+x_2^2+x_3^2)^{-1/2}\bigr)\\ \amp= MG \cdot \underbrace{2x_1}_{\text{inner deriv.}} \cdot \Bigl(-\tfrac{1}{2}\Bigr) (x_1^2+x_2^2+x_3^2)^{-3/2}\\ \amp= -\frac{MG\,x_1}{\|\mathbf{x}\|^3}. \end{align*}
    By symmetry the same calculation applies to \(x_2\) and \(x_3\text{,}\) giving
    \begin{equation*} \nabla f(\mathbf{x}) = -\frac{MG}{\|\mathbf{x}\|^3} \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{pmatrix} = \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}). \end{equation*}
    Hence \(f\) is an antiderivative of \(\mathbf{F}\text{,}\) and the gravitational field is conservative.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 4.3.8. Divergence and Curl.

    Let \(D \subseteq \R^n\) and let \(\mathbf{v}\colon D \to \R^n\) be a differentiable vector field.
    🔗
    The divergence of \(\mathbf{v}\) is the scalar field
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{div}\mathbf{v} \colon D \to \R, \qquad \mathbf{x} \mapsto \frac{\partial v_1}{\partial x_1}(\mathbf{x}) + \frac{\partial v_2}{\partial x_2}(\mathbf{x}) + \cdots + \frac{\partial v_n}{\partial x_n}(\mathbf{x}). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For \(n = 3\text{,}\) the curl of \(\mathbf{v}\) is the vector field
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{rot}\mathbf{v} \colon D \to \R^3, \qquad \mathbf{x} \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{\partial v_3}{\partial x_2}(\mathbf{x}) - \dfrac{\partial v_2}{\partial x_3}(\mathbf{x}) \\[6pt] \dfrac{\partial v_1}{\partial x_3}(\mathbf{x}) - \dfrac{\partial v_3}{\partial x_1}(\mathbf{x}) \\[6pt] \dfrac{\partial v_2}{\partial x_1}(\mathbf{x}) - \dfrac{\partial v_1}{\partial x_2}(\mathbf{x}) \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    It is also written \(\nabla \times \mathbf{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Physical interpretation. The divergence measures the net outward flux per unit volume at a point: a positive value indicates a source (fluid spreads outward), a negative value indicates a sink. The curl measures the circulation density: its magnitude gives the local rotation rate and its direction gives the axis of rotation.
    🔗
    🔗

    Map of Field Operations.

    The gradient, divergence, curl, and Laplacian form a coherent system of differential operators linking scalar and vector fields.
    🔗
    Operator Maps Formula / remark
    Gradient \(\nabla f\) scalar field \(\to\) vector field \(\nabla f = \Bigl(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_1},\ldots, \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}\Bigr)^{\!\top}\)
    Divergence \(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{v}\) vector field \(\to\) scalar field \(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{v} = \dfrac{\partial v_1}{\partial x_1}+\cdots+ \dfrac{\partial v_n}{\partial x_n}\)
    Curl \(\operatorname{rot}\mathbf{v}\) vector field \(\to\) vector field (only \(n=3\)) see Definition Definition 4.3.8
    Laplacian \(\Delta f\) scalar field \(\to\) scalar field \(\Delta f = \operatorname{div}(\nabla f) = \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_1^2}+\cdots+ \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_n^2}\)
    🔗

    Example 4.3.9. Computing Divergence and Curl.

    Let \(\mathbf{v}(x,y,z) = \begin{pmatrix} x^2 \\ yz \\ \sin y \end{pmatrix}\text{.}\) Compute \(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{v}\) and \(\operatorname{rot}\mathbf{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Divergence:
    \begin{align*} \operatorname{div}\mathbf{v} \amp= \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^2) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(yz) + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(\sin y)\\ \amp= 2x + z + 0 = 2x + z. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Curl:
    \begin{align*} \operatorname{rot}\mathbf{v} \amp= \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{\partial}{\partial y}(\sin y) - \dfrac{\partial}{\partial z}(yz) \\[6pt] \dfrac{\partial}{\partial z}(x^2) - \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}(\sin y) \\[6pt] \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}(yz) - \dfrac{\partial}{\partial y}(x^2) \end{pmatrix}\\ \amp= \begin{pmatrix} \cos y - y \\ 0 - 0 \\ 0 - 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos y - y \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.3.3 Line Integrals of Vector Fields

    We now integrate a vector field along a curve. The key difference from scalar line integrals is that we take the dot product of the field with the tangent vector of the curve: only the component of the field that is aligned with the motion contributes. The most important physical instance is work: the work done by a force field on a body moving along a path equals the vectorial line integral of the force along the path.
    🔗

    Definition 4.3.10. Line Integral of a Vector Field.

    Let \(D \subseteq \R^n\text{,}\) let \(\mathbf{x}\colon [a,b] \to D\) be a continuously differentiable curve, and let \(\mathbf{v}\colon D \to \R^n\) be a continuous vector field. The line integral of the vector field \(\mathbf{v}\) along \(\mathbf{x}\) is
    \begin{equation*} \int_{\mathbf{x}} \mathbf{v} \cdot d\mathbf{x} := \int_a^b \mathbf{v}\!\left(\mathbf{x}(t)\right) \cdot \dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) \, dt, \end{equation*}
    where the dot denotes the Euclidean inner product and \(\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{x}'(t)\) is the tangent vector.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 4.3.11. Physical Interpretation: Work Done by a Force Field.

    If \(\mathbf{F}\) is a force field and \(\mathbf{s}\colon [a,b] \to \R^n\) is the path of a moving body, the work done by the force on the body is
    \begin{equation*} W = \int_{\mathbf{s}} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{s} = \int_a^b \mathbf{F}\!\bigl(\mathbf{s}(t)\bigr) \cdot \dot{\mathbf{s}}(t) \, dt. \end{equation*}
    We can rewrite this by separating the speed and the unit tangent:
    \begin{equation*} W = \int_a^b \left[ \mathbf{F}\!\bigl(\mathbf{s}(t)\bigr) \cdot \frac{\dot{\mathbf{s}}(t)}{\|\dot{\mathbf{s}}(t)\|} \right] \|\dot{\mathbf{s}}(t)\| \, dt. \end{equation*}
    The bracketed factor is the orthogonal projection of \(\mathbf{F}\) onto the unit tangent: it is the component of the force acting along the direction of motion. Only this tangential component does work; the normal component is perpendicular to the displacement and contributes nothing.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 4.3.12. How to Compute a Vectorial Line Integral.

    To evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{x}} \mathbf{v} \cdot d\mathbf{x}\text{,}\) follow three steps.
    🔗
    Step 1: Parametrize the curve. Find a continuously differentiable parametrization \(\mathbf{x}\colon [a,b] \to \R^n\) whose trace is the desired path.
    🔗
    Step 2: Compute the tangent vector. Differentiate: \(\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{x}'(t)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 3: Form the dot product and integrate. Evaluate the ordinary integral
    \begin{equation*} \int_a^b \mathbf{v}\!\left(\mathbf{x}(t)\right) \cdot \dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) \, dt. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Note that the vectorial line integral is orientation-sensitive: reversing the direction of traversal changes the sign of the result, unlike the scalar line integral.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.3.13. Vectorial Line Integral Along a Quarter Circle.

    Compute the line integral of the vector field \(\mathbf{v}\colon \R^2 \to \R^2\text{,}\) \(\mathbf{v}(x,y) = \begin{pmatrix} x^2 \\ -xy \end{pmatrix}\text{,}\) along the quarter circle in the first quadrant, traversed from \((1,0)\) to \((0,1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Step 1: Parametrize.
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}\colon \left[0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right] \to \R^2, \qquad t \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} \cos t \\ \sin t \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Step 2: Tangent vector.
    \begin{equation*} \dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \begin{pmatrix} -\sin t \\ \cos t \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Step 3: Compute the dot product. Since \(x = \cos t\text{,}\) \(y = \sin t\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}\!\left(\mathbf{x}(t)\right) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos^2 t \\ -\cos t \sin t \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Therefore
    \begin{align*} \mathbf{v}\!\left(\mathbf{x}(t)\right) \cdot \dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) \amp= \cos^2 t \cdot (-\sin t) + (-\cos t \sin t) \cdot \cos t\\ \amp= -\sin t \cos^2 t - \sin t \cos^2 t = -2\sin t \cos^2 t. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Step 4: Integrate. Using the substitution \(u = \cos t\text{,}\) \(du = -\sin t \, dt\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} \int_{\mathbf{x}} \mathbf{v} \cdot d\mathbf{x} \amp= \int_0^{\pi/2} (-2\sin t \cos^2 t)\, dt\\ \amp= \int_1^0 2u^2 \, du = -\int_0^1 2u^2 \, du\\ \amp= -2 \left[\frac{u^3}{3}\right]_0^1 = -\frac{2}{3}. \end{align*}
    Reversing the orientation of the same quarter circle would change the sign of the integral to \(\frac{2}{3}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 4.3.14. Fundamental Theorem for Line Integrals.

    Let \(D \subseteq \R^n\text{,}\) let \(\mathbf{v}\colon D \to \R^n\) be a vector field, and let \(f\colon D \to \R\) be a differentiable antiderivative of \(\mathbf{v}\) (i.e.\ \(\nabla f = \mathbf{v}\)). Then for every continuously differentiable curve \(\mathbf{x}\colon [a,b] \to D\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \int_{\mathbf{x}} \mathbf{v} \cdot d\mathbf{x} = f\!\left(\mathbf{x}(b)\right) - f\!\left(\mathbf{x}(a)\right). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Two important consequences follow immediately.
    🔗
    Closed curves give zero. If \(\mathbf{x}(a) = \mathbf{x}(b)\) (i.e.\ the curve is closed), then
    \begin{equation*} \int_{\mathbf{x}} \mathbf{v} \cdot d\mathbf{x} = 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Path independence. The integral depends only on the start and end points, not on the path taken between them: if \(\mathbf{x}\) and \(\mathbf{y}\) are two curves in \(D\) with the same start and end points, then
    \begin{equation*} \int_{\mathbf{x}} \mathbf{v} \cdot d\mathbf{x} = \int_{\mathbf{y}} \mathbf{v} \cdot d\mathbf{y}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    Consider the composite function \(g(t) = f(\mathbf{x}(t))\) on \([a,b]\text{.}\) By the chain rule,
    \begin{equation*} g'(t) = \nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}(t)\right) \cdot \dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{v}\!\left(\mathbf{x}(t)\right) \cdot \dot{\mathbf{x}}(t). \end{equation*}
    Integrating from \(a\) to \(b\) and applying the ordinary fundamental theorem of calculus gives
    \begin{equation*} \int_a^b \mathbf{v}\!\left(\mathbf{x}(t)\right) \cdot \dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)\,dt = \int_a^b g'(t)\,dt = g(b)-g(a) = f\!\left(\mathbf{x}(b)\right)-f\!\left(\mathbf{x}(a)\right). \end{equation*}
    This is exactly the stated formula.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 4.3.16. Existence and Computation of Antiderivatives.

    The fundamental theorem raises three natural questions.
    🔗
    1. Does every continuous vector field have an antiderivative?
    🔗
    No. A simple counterexample is the rotation field \(\mathbf{v}(x,y) = \begin{pmatrix} -y \\ x \end{pmatrix}\text{.}\) Suppose \(f\) were an antiderivative. Then \(\partial f/\partial x = -y\text{,}\) so integrating in \(x\text{:}\) \(f(x,y) = -yx + g(y)\text{.}\) Differentiating in \(y\text{:}\) \(\partial f/\partial y = -x + g'(y) \overset{!}{=} x\text{,}\) which forces \(g'(y) = 2x\text{.}\) But \(g\) depends only on \(y\text{,}\) so this is a contradiction. The rotation field is not conservative.
    🔗
    2. When does an antiderivative exist?
    🔗
    A necessary condition (in \(\R^3\)) is that the curl vanishes:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v} \text{ has an antiderivative} \implies \operatorname{rot}\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}. \end{equation*}
    This follows from Schwarz’s theorem on the equality of mixed partial derivatives. For a continuously differentiable vector field on a simply connected domain, the converse also holds: \(\operatorname{rot}\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\) implies \(\mathbf{v}\) is conservative.
    🔗
    In the planar case \(\mathbf{v}(x,y) = (P(x,y),Q(x,y))\text{,}\) the corresponding condition is \(\partial Q/\partial x = \partial P/\partial y\text{.}\) This is the two-dimensional version students will use most often in examples.
    🔗
    3. How to find an antiderivative, if one exists?
    🔗
    Use successive integration: integrate the first component equation in \(x_1\text{,}\) then determine the unknown function of the remaining variables from the second component equation, and so on. The following example illustrates the method in three variables.
    🔗
    Let \(\mathbf{v}(x,y,z) = \begin{pmatrix} e^x y + 1 \\ e^x + z \\ y \end{pmatrix}\text{.}\) We seek \(f\) with \(\nabla f = \mathbf{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Integrate \(\partial f/\partial x = e^x y + 1\) in \(x\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} f(x,y,z) = e^x y + x + g(y,z). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Use \(\partial f/\partial y = e^x + z\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} e^x + \frac{\partial g}{\partial y}(y,z) = e^x + z \implies \frac{\partial g}{\partial y} = z \implies g(y,z) = zy + h(z). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Use \(\partial f/\partial z = y\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} y + h'(z) = y \implies h'(z) = 0 \implies h(z) = C \in \R. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Therefore
    \begin{equation*} f(x,y,z) = e^x y + x + zy + C, \qquad C \in \R. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Example 4.3.17. Work Along Two Paths in a Conservative Field.

    The force field \(\mathbf{v}\colon \R^2 \to \R^2\) is defined by
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}(x,y) = \begin{pmatrix} x^2 + y \\ x + \cos y \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    The curve \(\mathbf{s}_1\) runs along the \(x\)-axis from \((1,0)\) to \((-1,0)\text{,}\) and \(\mathbf{s}_2\) connects the same two points along the upper unit semicircle.
    🔗
    1. Compute the work done along \(\mathbf{s}_1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Compute the work done along \(\mathbf{s}_2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Find an antiderivative \(F\colon \R^2 \to \R\) of \(\mathbf{v}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Solution.
    Part (a): Work along \(\mathbf{s}_1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Parametrize the straight path from \((1,0)\) to \((-1,0)\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{s}_1\colon [-1,1] \to \R^2, \qquad t \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} -t \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    (At \(t=-1\) we are at \((1,0)\text{;}\) at \(t=1\) at \((-1,0)\text{.}\)) The tangent vector is \(\dot{\mathbf{s}}_1(t) = \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\text{.}\) The field along the path:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_1(t)\right) = \begin{pmatrix} (-t)^2 + 0 \\ -t + \cos 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} t^2 \\ -t + 1 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Therefore
    \begin{align*} \int_{\mathbf{s}_1} \mathbf{v} \cdot d\mathbf{s}_1 \amp= \int_{-1}^1 \begin{pmatrix} t^2 \\ -t+1 \end{pmatrix} \cdot \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \, dt\\ \amp= \int_{-1}^1 (-t^2) \, dt = \left[-\frac{t^3}{3}\right]_{-1}^1 = -\frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{3} = -\frac{2}{3}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Part (c): Antiderivative of \(\mathbf{v}\text{.}\) (We solve (c) before (b) since (b) uses the result.)
    🔗
    We search for \(F\) with \(\nabla F = \mathbf{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Integrate \(\partial F/\partial x = x^2 + y\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} F(x,y) = \frac{x^3}{3} + xy + C(y). \end{equation*}
    Use \(\partial F/\partial y = x + \cos y\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} x + C'(y) = x + \cos y \implies C'(y) = \cos y \implies C(y) = \sin y + C_0, \quad C_0 \in \R. \end{equation*}
    Therefore
    \begin{equation*} F(x,y) = \frac{x^3}{3} + xy + \sin y + C_0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Part (b): Work along \(\mathbf{s}_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Since \(F\) is an antiderivative, \(\mathbf{v}\) is conservative and the integral depends only on the endpoints, not on the particular path. So we do not need a separate parametrization of the semicircle. By the fundamental theorem:
    \begin{align*} \int_{\mathbf{s}_2} \mathbf{v} \cdot d\mathbf{s}_2 \amp= F(-1,0) - F(1,0)\\ \amp= \left(\frac{(-1)^3}{3} + (-1)(0) + \sin 0 + C_0\right) - \left(\frac{1^3}{3} + 1 \cdot 0 + \sin 0 + C_0\right)\\ \amp= -\frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{3} = -\frac{2}{3}. \end{align*}
    As expected, both paths give the same work value \(-\tfrac{2}{3}\text{.}\)

    Exercises Check Your Understanding

    1. Path Independence.
    If a vector field is conservative, what determines the value of the line integral between two points?
    🔗
    • Only the endpoints
    • Correct. In a conservative field, the integral is path independent and equals a boundary evaluation of a potential.
    • Only the length of the path
    • Incorrect. Different paths of the same length can still have different integrals in nonconservative fields.
    • Only whether the path is closed
    • Incorrect. The endpoints matter; closed curves are only a special case.
    • Only the maximum value of the field along the path
    • Incorrect. That does not determine a line integral.
    🔗
    2. Integrate a Constant Field Along a Segment.
    Compute the line integral of the constant vector field
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix} \end{equation*}
    along the line segment from \((0,0)\) to \((1,3)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    A potential for this field is
    \begin{equation*} F(x,y)=x+2y. \end{equation*}
    Therefore, by the fundamental theorem for line integrals,
    \begin{equation*} \int_C \mathbf{v}\cdot d\mathbf{s} = F(1,3)-F(0,0) = (1+2\cdot 3)-0 = 7. \end{equation*}
