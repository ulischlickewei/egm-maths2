    Section 1.3 Eigenvalues, Diagonalization, and Quadratic Forms

    Our final section in Linear Algebra brings together three closely connected ideas. We begin with eigenvalues and eigenvectors, which capture the directions along which a matrix acts by pure scaling. We then use them to diagonalize matrices — a technique that dramatically simplifies the computation of matrix powers. Finally, we study quadratic forms, which describe how a symmetric matrix assigns a real value to each vector. Quadratic forms will reappear in Section 2.2, where they determine whether a critical point of a function is a minimum, a maximum, or a saddle point.
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.1 Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

    When a vector is multiplied by a square matrix, usually both the length and the direction will change. However, for some special non-zero vectors the effect is particularly simple: multiplication by the matrix changes only the size, while the direction remains the same. Such vectors are called eigenvectors, and the corresponding scaling factors are called eigenvalues.
    🔗
    Eigenvalues and eigenvectors play an important role in many applications. In mechanics they describe principal modes of deformation or vibration, in electrical engineering they appear in state-space models, and in data science they are used, for example, in principal component analysis. In this section we introduce the basic definitions, explain how eigenvalues can be computed, and discuss some first important examples.
    🔗

    Example 1.3.1. Distinguished Directions of a Matrix.

    Consider the matrix
    \begin{equation*} A= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 4 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \end{equation*}
    and the vectors
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{u}= \begin{pmatrix} 1\\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{v}= \begin{pmatrix} 1\\ -4 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{w}= \begin{pmatrix} 0\\ 1 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We compute
    \begin{equation*} A\mathbf{u} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 4 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1\\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1\\ 6 \end{pmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} A\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 4 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1\\ -4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1\\ -4 \end{pmatrix} = \mathbf{v}, \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} A\mathbf{w} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 4 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0\\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0\\ 2 \end{pmatrix} = 2\mathbf{w}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The vector \(\mathbf{u}\) changes its direction under multiplication by \(A\text{.}\) In contrast, \(\mathbf{v}\) keeps its direction and even remains unchanged, while \(\mathbf{w}\) keeps its direction and is stretched by the factor \(2\text{.}\) The following graphics visualize these three cases.
    🔗
    These pictures show that some vectors are mapped to scalar multiples of themselves when multiplied by \(A\)You. This is exactly the phenomenon captured by the notion of eigenvalues and eigenvectors.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.2. Eigenvalue and Eigenvector.

    Let \(A\) be an \(n\times n\)-matrix. A non-zero vector \(\mathbf{v}\in\R^n\) is called an eigenvector of \(A\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda\in\R\) if
    \begin{equation*} A\mathbf{v}=\lambda \mathbf{v}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    The equation \(A\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}\) means that multiplication by \(A\) has the same effect on \(\mathbf{v}\) as multiplication by the scalar \(\lambda\text{.}\) Hence \(A\) sends the vector \(\mathbf{v}\) to a vector parallel to \(\mathbf{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 1.3.3. Two Eigenvectors of a Matrix.

    For the matrix
    \begin{equation*} A= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 4 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \end{equation*}
    from the previous example, we have already seen that
    \begin{equation*} A\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}=1\mathbf{v} \qquad\text{for}\qquad \mathbf{v}= \begin{pmatrix} 1\\ -4 \end{pmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} A\mathbf{w}=2\mathbf{w} \qquad\text{for}\qquad \mathbf{w}= \begin{pmatrix} 0\\ 1 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Therefore \(\mathbf{v}\) is an eigenvector with eigenvalue \(1\text{,}\) and \(\mathbf{w}\) is an eigenvector with eigenvalue \(2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.4. Eigenspace.

    Let \(A\) be an \(n\times n\)-matrix and let \(\lambda\) be an eigenvalue of \(A\text{.}\) The set
    \begin{equation*} H_{\lambda} = \left\{ \mathbf{v}\in\R^n \mid A\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v} \right\} \end{equation*}
    is called the eigenspace corresponding to \(\lambda\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The eigenspace contains all eigenvectors corresponding to \(\lambda\) and also the zero vector. The zero vector itself is never an eigenvector, because eigenvectors must be non-zero by definition.
    🔗

    Example 1.3.5. Testing Whether a Given Vector Is an Eigenvector.

    Consider
    \begin{equation*} A= \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -4\\ -1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{v}= \begin{pmatrix} -4\\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{w}= \begin{pmatrix} 3\\ 1 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    To test whether a vector is an eigenvector, we multiply it by \(A\) and check whether the result is a scalar multiple of the original vector.
    🔗
    First,
    \begin{equation*} A\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -4\\ -1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} -4\\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -12\\ 3 \end{pmatrix} = 3 \begin{pmatrix} -4\\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = 3\mathbf{v}. \end{equation*}
    Hence \(\mathbf{v}\) is an eigenvector of \(A\) with eigenvalue \(3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Next,
    \begin{equation*} A\mathbf{w} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -4\\ -1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 3\\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2\\ -4 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    This vector is not a scalar multiple of \(\mathbf{w}=\begin{pmatrix}3\\1\end{pmatrix}\text{.}\) Therefore \(\mathbf{w}\) is not an eigenvector of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    After having studied how to check whether a given vector is an eigenvector or not of a matrix, we next deal with the question how to find the eigenvectors to a given eigenvalue. To answer this question we will frame the problem of determining the eigenspace to a given eigenvalue as a linear system. This is the content of the following theorem.
    🔗

    Theorem 1.3.6. Linear System for Eigenvectors.

    Let \(A\) be an \(n\times n\)-matrix and let \(\lambda\in\R\text{.}\) Then the following statements are equivalent.
    🔗
    • \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of \(A\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • The homogeneous linear system
      \begin{equation*} (A-\lambda I_n)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0} \end{equation*}
      has a non-trivial solution.
      🔗
      🔗
    In this case,
    \begin{equation*} H_{\lambda} = \left\{ \mathbf{x}\in\R^n \mid (A-\lambda I_n)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0} \right\}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    By definition, \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of \(A\) if and only if there exists a non-zero vector \(\mathbf{v}\) such that
    \begin{equation*} A\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}. \end{equation*}
    Subtracting \(\lambda\mathbf{v}\) from both sides gives
    \begin{equation*} A\mathbf{v}-\lambda\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Since \(\lambda\mathbf{v}=\lambda I_n\mathbf{v}\text{,}\) where \(I_n=\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & \ddots & \vdots \\ \vdots & \ddots & \ddots & 0 \\ 0 & \cdots & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) is the identity matrix, this is equivalent to
    \begin{equation*} (A-\lambda I_n)\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}. \end{equation*}
    Thus \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue exactly if the system \((A-\lambda I_n)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\) has a non-trivial solution. The set of all solutions of this system is precisely the eigenspace \(H_{\lambda}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    This theorem is important because it turns an eigenvalue problem into a familiar problem from the previous section: solving a homogeneous linear system.
    🔗

    Example 1.3.7. Finding the Eigenspace for a Given Eigenvalue.

    Again consider
    \begin{equation*} A= \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -4\\ -1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    We show that \(\lambda=-2\) is an eigenvalue and determine the corresponding eigenspace.
    🔗
    We have to solve
    \begin{equation*} (A-(-2)I_2)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}, \end{equation*}
    that is,
    \begin{equation*} (A+2I_2)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}. \end{equation*}
    Since
    \begin{equation*} A+2I_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & -4\\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    the augmented coefficient matrix is
    \begin{equation*} \left( \begin{array}{cc|c} 4 & -4 & 0\\ -1 & 1 & 0 \end{array} \right) \sim \left( \begin{array}{cc|c} 1 & -1 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{array} \right). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Hence the system is equivalent to
    \begin{equation*} x_1-x_2=0, \end{equation*}
    so \(x_1=x_2=t\) with \(t\in\R\text{.}\) Therefore
    \begin{equation*} H_{-2} = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} t\\ t \end{pmatrix} \;\middle|\; t\in\R \right\} = \left\{ t \begin{pmatrix} 1\\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \;\middle|\; t\in\R \right\}. \end{equation*}
    Every non-zero vector in this eigenspace is an eigenvector corresponding to \(\lambda=-2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The eigenvalue \(\lambda=0\) is possible. In that case there exists a non-zero vector \(\mathbf{v}\) such that
    \begin{equation*} A\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}. \end{equation*}
    So \(0\) is an eigenvalue exactly if the homogeneous system \(A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\) has a non-trivial solution. By the invertibility criteria from the previous section, this means that \(A\) is not invertible.
    🔗

    Example 1.3.8. Eigenvalues of an Upper Triangular Matrix.

    Consider the upper triangular matrix
    \begin{equation*} A= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 3\\ 0 & 2 & -1\\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    We determine its eigenvalues.
    🔗
    The number \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue if and only if the system
    \begin{equation*} (A-\lambda I_3)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0} \end{equation*}
    has a non-trivial solution. Now
    \begin{equation*} A-\lambda I_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 1-\lambda & 0 & 3\\ 0 & 2-\lambda & -1\\ 0 & 0 & -\lambda \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    This matrix is still upper triangular.
    🔗
    For the system to have a non-trivial solution, the matrix \(A-\lambda I_3\) must fail to have a pivot in every column. Since the diagonal entries are \(1-\lambda\text{,}\) \(2-\lambda\text{,}\) and \(-\lambda\text{,}\) this happens exactly when at least one of them is zero. Hence
    \begin{equation*} \lambda=1,\qquad \lambda=2,\qquad \lambda=0 \end{equation*}
    are the eigenvalues of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 1.3.9. Eigenvalues of Triangular Matrices.

    Let \(A\) be an upper or lower triangular \(n\times n\)-matrix. Then the eigenvalues of \(A\) are exactly the diagonal entries of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    The matrix \(A-\lambda I_n\) is again triangular, and its diagonal entries are obtained by subtracting \(\lambda\) from the diagonal entries of \(A\text{.}\) A triangular matrix is invertible exactly if all diagonal entries are non-zero. Therefore \(A-\lambda I_n\) is not invertible exactly when one of these diagonal entries is zero. By the previous theorem, this is equivalent to \(\lambda\) being an eigenvalue.
    🔗
    🔗
    In practice, this theorem is very useful. For triangular matrices the eigenvalues can be read off immediately from the main diagonal, without any further calculation.
    🔗
    We have seen how to check if a given vector is an eigenvector of a matrix (how?). Also, we have seen how to find the eigenspace corresponding to a given eigenvalue (how?). Next, we will see how to find the eigenvalues of a matrix.
    🔗

    Theorem 1.3.10. Characteristic Polynomial.

    Let \(A\) be an \(n\times n\)-matrix. The polynomial
    \begin{equation*} p_A(\lambda)=\det(A-\lambda I_n) \end{equation*}
    is called the characteristic polynomial of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    A scalar \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of \(A\) if and only if
    \begin{equation*} p_A(\lambda)=0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    By the theorem on eigenvectors, \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue exactly if the homogeneous system
    \begin{equation*} (A-\lambda I_n)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0} \end{equation*}
    has a non-trivial solution. By the invertibility criteria, this is equivalent to \(A-\lambda I_n\) not being invertible. For square matrices this happens exactly when
    \begin{equation*} \det(A-\lambda I_n)=0. \end{equation*}
    Hence the zeros of the characteristic polynomial are precisely the eigenvalues of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.3.11.

    This is where the determinant tools from the previous section become essential: to find eigenvalues, we first build the matrix \(A-\lambda I_n\) and then compute its determinant. In other words, the new topic of eigenvalues builds directly on the determinant techniques from Section 1.2.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.3.12. Computing Eigenvalues via the Characteristic Polynomial.

    Consider
    \begin{equation*} A= \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3\\ 3 & -6 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    We determine the eigenvalues of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The characteristic polynomial is
    \begin{equation*} p_A(\lambda) = \det \begin{pmatrix} 2-\lambda & 3\\ 3 & -6-\lambda \end{pmatrix} = (2-\lambda)(-6-\lambda)-9. \end{equation*}
    Expanding gives
    \begin{equation*} p_A(\lambda)=\lambda^2+4\lambda-21. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Therefore the eigenvalues are the zeros of this polynomial:
    \begin{equation*} \lambda^2+4\lambda-21=0. \end{equation*}
    Using the quadratic formula, we obtain
    \begin{equation*} \lambda_{1,2} = \frac{-4\pm\sqrt{16+84}}{2} = \frac{-4\pm 10}{2}. \end{equation*}
    Hence
    \begin{equation*} \lambda_1=3, \qquad \lambda_2=-7. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.13. Algebraic and Geometric Multiplicity.

    Let \(\lambda_0\) be an eigenvalue of the matrix \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If the characteristic polynomial contains the factor \((\lambda-\lambda_0)^k\text{,}\) but not the factor \((\lambda-\lambda_0)^{k+1}\text{,}\) then \(k\) is called the algebraic multiplicity of \(\lambda_0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The geometric multiplicity of \(\lambda_0\) is the maximum number of linearly independent vectors in the eigenspace \(H_{\lambda_0}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The geometric multiplicity tells us how many linearly independent eigenvectors belong to an eigenvalue. It is always at least \(1\) and never larger than the algebraic multiplicity.
    🔗

    Example 1.3.14. A Repeated Eigenvalue.

    Consider
    \begin{equation*} A= \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -18\\ 2 & -9 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    We determine its eigenvalues and eigenvectors.
    🔗
    The characteristic polynomial is
    \begin{equation*} p_A(\lambda) = \det \begin{pmatrix} 3-\lambda & -18\\ 2 & -9-\lambda \end{pmatrix} = (3-\lambda)(-9-\lambda)+36 = \lambda^2+6\lambda+9 = (\lambda+3)^2. \end{equation*}
    Thus \(\lambda=-3\) is the only eigenvalue, and its algebraic multiplicity is \(2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    To find the eigenspace, we solve
    \begin{equation*} (A+3I_2)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}. \end{equation*}
    Since
    \begin{equation*} A+3I_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & -18\\ 2 & -6 \end{pmatrix} \sim \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -3\\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    we get the equation
    \begin{equation*} x_1-3x_2=0. \end{equation*}
    Hence
    \begin{equation*} H_{-3} = \left\{ t \begin{pmatrix} 3\\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \;\middle|\; t\in\R \right\}. \end{equation*}
    All eigenvectors in \(H_{-3}\) are multiples of \(\begin{pmatrix}3\\1\end{pmatrix}\text{,}\) so we can extract only one linearly independent eigenvector from this eigenspace. Therefore the geometric multiplicity is \(1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    This example shows that algebraic and geometric multiplicity need not coincide.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.3.15. A Rotation Matrix.

    Consider the matrix
    \begin{equation*} A= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1\\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    which describes a rotation by \(\frac{\pi}{2}\) around the origin in the counter-clockwise direction.
    🔗
    Over the real numbers, this matrix has no eigenvectors. Indeed, a rotation by \(90^\circ\) changes the direction of every non-zero real vector, so no non-zero vector can satisfy \(A\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}\) with \(\lambda\in\R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Algebraically, the characteristic polynomial is
    \begin{equation*} p_A(\lambda) = \det \begin{pmatrix} -\lambda & -1\\ 1 & -\lambda \end{pmatrix} = \lambda^2+1. \end{equation*}
    This polynomial has no real zero, hence \(A\) has no real eigenvalue.
    🔗
    Over the complex numbers, however, the equation
    \begin{equation*} \lambda^2+1=0 \end{equation*}
    has the solutions
    \begin{equation*} \lambda=i, \qquad \lambda=-i. \end{equation*}
    Thus the matrix has complex eigenvalues. This example illustrates that real matrices need not have real eigenvalues.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.3.16.

    This example shows an important limitation: a real matrix need not have enough real eigenvalues and eigenvectors to be diagonalized over \(\R\text{.}\) When we study diagonalization in the next subsection, we will therefore pay attention to whether the characteristic polynomial actually has real zeros.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.3.17. Engineering Interpretation.

    In many engineering models, a matrix describes how a system transforms states or forces. If \(\mathbf{v}\) is an eigenvector, then the action of the matrix on this state is especially simple: the state keeps its direction and is only scaled by \(\lambda\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For example, in a mechanical system an eigenvector may represent a preferred vibration mode, and the corresponding eigenvalue can be related to amplification or damping. In stress analysis, eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix describe principal directions, that is, directions in which no shear component appears. This is one reason why eigenvalues are fundamental in applied mathematics and engineering.
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 1.3.18. Powers of a Matrix and the Inverse Matrix.

    Let \(\mathbf{v}\) be an eigenvector of \(A\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda\text{.}\)
    🔗
    • For every \(k\in\N\text{,}\)
      \begin{equation*} A^k\mathbf{v}=\lambda^k\mathbf{v}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(A\) is invertible, then \(\lambda\neq 0\) and
      \begin{equation*} A^{-1}\mathbf{v}=\lambda^{-1}\mathbf{v}. \end{equation*}
      Hence \(\lambda^{-1}\) is an eigenvalue of \(A^{-1}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    The first claim follows by repeated application of the relation \(A\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}\text{.}\) For example,
    \begin{equation*} A^3\mathbf{v} = A(A(A\mathbf{v})) = A(A(\lambda\mathbf{v})) = A(\lambda A\mathbf{v}) = \lambda A(\lambda\mathbf{v}) = \lambda^2 A\mathbf{v} = \lambda^3\mathbf{v}. \end{equation*}
    The general case is proved in the same way by induction.
    🔗
    For the second claim, apply \(A^{-1}\) to the equation \(A\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}\text{.}\) This gives
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v} = A^{-1}(\lambda\mathbf{v}) = \lambda A^{-1}\mathbf{v}. \end{equation*}
    Since \(A\) is invertible, the eigenvalue \(\lambda\) cannot be zero. Dividing by \(\lambda\) yields
    \begin{equation*} A^{-1}\mathbf{v}=\lambda^{-1}\mathbf{v}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    We summarize the procedure for computing eigenvalues and eigenvectors.
    🔗

    Remark 1.3.19. Procedure for Determining Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors.

    Let \(A\) be an \(n\times n\)-matrix.
    🔗
    1. Compute the characteristic polynomial
      \begin{equation*} p_A(\lambda)=\det(A-\lambda I_n). \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Determine the zeros of \(p_A\text{.}\) These are the eigenvalues of \(A\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. For each eigenvalue \(\lambda_0\text{,}\) solve the homogeneous system
      \begin{equation*} (A-\lambda_0 I_n)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0} \end{equation*}
      to obtain the eigenspace \(H_{\lambda_0}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    • \(4\lambda\mathbf{v}\)
    • Incorrect. Repeated application multiplies by \(\lambda\) each time, so the factor is \(\lambda^4\text{,}\) not \(4\lambda\text{.}\)
    • \(\lambda^4\mathbf{v}\)
    • Correct. Applying \(A\) four times multiplies the eigenvector by \(\lambda\) four times.
    • \(A^4\lambda\mathbf{v}\)
    • Incorrect. The expression can be simplified much further using the eigenvector property.
    • \(\lambda\mathbf{v}^4\)
    • Incorrect. Vectors are not exponentiated in this way.

    3. Find Eigenvalues and Eigenspaces.
    Determine the eigenvalues and eigenspaces of
    \begin{equation*} A= \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1\\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    We compute the characteristic polynomial:
    \begin{equation*} p_A(\lambda) = \det\begin{pmatrix}4-\lambda & 1\\ 2 & 3-\lambda\end{pmatrix} = (4-\lambda)(3-\lambda)-2 = \lambda^2-7\lambda+10. \end{equation*}
    Factoring gives
    \begin{equation*} p_A(\lambda)=(\lambda-5)(\lambda-2), \end{equation*}
    so the eigenvalues are \(\lambda_1=5\) and \(\lambda_2=2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For \(\lambda=5\text{,}\) we solve
    \begin{equation*} (A-5I_2)\mathbf{x} = \begin{pmatrix}-1 & 1\\ 2 & -2\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{x} =\mathbf{0}. \end{equation*}
    This gives \(x_2=x_1\text{,}\) hence
    \begin{equation*} H_5 = \left\{ t\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix} \;\middle|\; t\in\R \right\}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For \(\lambda=2\text{,}\) we solve
    \begin{equation*} (A-2I_2)\mathbf{x} = \begin{pmatrix}2 & 1\\ 2 & 1\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{x} =\mathbf{0}. \end{equation*}
    This gives \(2x_1+x_2=0\text{,}\) so \(x_2=-2x_1\text{.}\) Therefore
    \begin{equation*} H_2 = \left\{ t\begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\end{pmatrix} \;\middle|\; t\in\R \right\}. \end{equation*}

    Subsection 1.3.2 Diagonalization of Matrices

    Example 1.3.20. Powers of a Diagonal Matrix.

    Consider the diagonal matrix
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    For any \(k \in \N\text{,}\) its \(k\)-th power is
    \begin{equation*} A^k = \begin{pmatrix} 5^k & 0 \\ 0 & 3^k \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    This follows immediately from matrix multiplication: each diagonal entry is raised to the power \(k\) independently.
    🔗
    🔗
    The example above shows why diagonal matrices are so attractive: their powers are immediate to compute. This raises a natural question: can we use this simple structure to compute powers of more general matrices?
    🔗
    The answer is yes, provided the matrix can be diagonalized, that is, written in the form \(A = PDP^{-1}\) with an invertible matrix \(P\) and a diagonal matrix \(D\text{.}\) As we will see, the columns of \(P\) are eigenvectors of \(A\text{,}\) and the diagonal entries of \(D\) are the corresponding eigenvalues.
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.21. Diagonalizable Matrix.

    An \(n \times n\) matrix \(A\) is called diagonalizable if there exists an invertible matrix \(P\) and a diagonal matrix \(D\) such that
    \begin{equation*} A = P D P^{-1}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    If \(A = PDP^{-1}\text{,}\) then a simple induction gives
    \begin{equation*} A^k = (PDP^{-1})^k = P D^k P^{-1}, \end{equation*}
    since the intermediate factors \(P^{-1}P = I\) cancel. Computing \(A^k\) then reduces to computing \(D^k\text{,}\) which is trivial.
    🔗

    Theorem 1.3.22. Criterion for Diagonalizability.

    Let \(A\) be an \(n \times n\) matrix.
    🔗
    1. \(A\) is diagonalizable if and only if there exist \(n\) linearly independent eigenvectors of \(A\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. If \(A = PDP^{-1}\text{,}\) then the columns of \(P\) are linearly independent eigenvectors of \(A\text{,}\) and the diagonal entries of \(D\) are the corresponding eigenvalues.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. If the characteristic polynomial of \(A\) splits into linear factors over \(\R\text{,}\) then \(A\) is diagonalizable if and only if for every eigenvalue \(\lambda_i\text{,}\) its algebraic multiplicity equals its geometric multiplicity.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    For part (1), first assume that \(A\) is diagonalizable, so \(A=PDP^{-1}\) for some invertible matrix \(P\text{.}\) Multiplying by \(P\) on the right gives \(AP=PD\text{.}\) If \(\mathbf{p}_i\) denotes the \(i\)-th column of \(P\) and \(\lambda_i\) the corresponding diagonal entry of \(D\text{,}\) then the \(i\)-th column of \(AP=PD\) reads \(A\mathbf{p}_i=\lambda_i\mathbf{p}_i\text{.}\) So the columns of \(P\) are eigenvectors of \(A\text{.}\) Because \(P\) is invertible, these columns are linearly independent, so we obtain \(n\) linearly independent eigenvectors.
    🔗
    Conversely, suppose that \(\mathbf{v}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{v}_n\) are linearly independent eigenvectors with corresponding eigenvalues \(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n\text{.}\) Form the matrix
    \begin{equation*} P=\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{v}_1 & \cdots & \mathbf{v}_n \end{pmatrix} \qquad\text{and}\qquad D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n). \end{equation*}
    Then
    \begin{equation*} AP = \begin{pmatrix} A\mathbf{v}_1 & \cdots & A\mathbf{v}_n \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1\mathbf{v}_1 & \cdots & \lambda_n\mathbf{v}_n \end{pmatrix} = PD. \end{equation*}
    Since the columns of \(P\) are linearly independent, \(P\) is invertible. Multiplying \(AP=PD\) on the right by \(P^{-1}\) gives \(A=PDP^{-1}\text{,}\) so \(A\) is diagonalizable.
    🔗
    Part (2) follows from the identity \(AP=PD\text{.}\) If \(\mathbf{p}_i\) denotes the \(i\)-th column of \(P\) and \(\lambda_i\) the corresponding diagonal entry of \(D\text{,}\) then the \(i\)-th column of \(AP=PD\) reads \(A\mathbf{p}_i=\lambda_i\mathbf{p}_i\text{.}\) So each column of \(P\) is an eigenvector with the matching eigenvalue.
    🔗
    For part (3), assume that the characteristic polynomial splits over \(\R\text{.}\) Then the sum of the algebraic multiplicities of all eigenvalues is \(n\text{.}\) For each eigenvalue, the geometric multiplicity is at most the algebraic multiplicity. Hence equality for every eigenvalue is equivalent to having a total of \(n\) linearly independent eigenvectors. By part (1), this is exactly the condition for diagonalizability.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.3.23.

    The extra hypothesis in part (3) matters. For example, the rotation matrix from the previous subsection has no real eigenvalues, so it cannot be diagonalized over \(\R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    This criterion gives a practical recipe: compute all eigenvalues, determine their algebraic and geometric multiplicities, and check whether the multiplicities match. If they do — and if together we collect \(n\) linearly independent eigenvectors — then \(A\) is diagonalizable and we can form \(P\) and \(D\) directly from the eigenvectors and eigenvalues.
    🔗

    Example 1.3.24. Computing a Power via Diagonalization.

    Let
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 7 & 2 \\ -4 & 1 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    We want to compute \(A^k\) for arbitrary \(k \in \N\text{.}\) We are given that \(A = PDP^{-1}\) with
    \begin{equation*} P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & -2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad D = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    First, we compute \(P^{-1}\) using the \(2 \times 2\) inverse formula:
    \begin{equation*} \det(P) = 1 \cdot (-2) - 1 \cdot (-1) = -1, \qquad P^{-1} = \frac{1}{-1} \begin{pmatrix} -2 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ -1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Now we use \(A^k = P D^k P^{-1}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} A^k = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & -2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 5^k & 0 \\ 0 & 3^k \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ -1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Carrying out the multiplications:
    \begin{equation*} A^k = \begin{pmatrix} 5^k & 3^k \\ -5^k & -2 \cdot 3^k \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ -1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \cdot 5^k - 3^k & 5^k - 3^k \\ -2 \cdot 5^k + 2 \cdot 3^k & -5^k + 2 \cdot 3^k \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.3.25. Diagonalizing a \(3 \times 3\) Matrix.

    Find matrices \(P\) and \(D\) such that \(B = PDP^{-1}\text{,}\) where
    \begin{equation*} B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & -2 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Step 1: Characteristic polynomial. We compute
    \begin{equation*} p_B(\lambda) = \det(B - \lambda I) = \det \begin{pmatrix} -\lambda & 0 & -2 \\ 1 & 2-\lambda & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 3-\lambda \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Expanding along the second column (which has two zeros):
    \begin{equation*} p_B(\lambda) = (2-\lambda) \det \begin{pmatrix} -\lambda & -2 \\ 1 & 3-\lambda \end{pmatrix} = (2-\lambda)\bigl[-\lambda(3-\lambda)+2\bigr] = (2-\lambda)(\lambda^2 - 3\lambda + 2) = -(\lambda-1)(\lambda-2)^2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Step 2: Eigenvalues. The eigenvalues are \(\lambda_1 = 1\) (algebraic multiplicity 1) and \(\lambda_2 = 2\) (algebraic multiplicity 2).
    🔗
    Step 3: Eigenspaces. For \(\lambda_1 = 1\text{,}\) we solve \((B - I)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 & -2 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \sim \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    The free variable is \(x_3\text{,}\) giving the eigenvector \(\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For \(\lambda_2 = 2\text{,}\) we solve \((B - 2I)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \begin{pmatrix} -2 & 0 & -2 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \sim \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    The free variables are \(x_2\) and \(x_3\text{,}\) giving two linearly independent eigenvectors
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{v}_3 = \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    The geometric multiplicity of \(\lambda_2 = 2\) is 2, matching the algebraic multiplicity.
    🔗
    Conclusion. Since both multiplicities match for every eigenvalue, \(B\) is diagonalizable. Placing the eigenvectors as columns of \(P\) and the corresponding eigenvalues on the diagonal of \(D\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} P = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad D = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.3.26. A Non-Diagonalizable Matrix.

    Consider
    \begin{equation*} C = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    The characteristic polynomial is \(p_C(\lambda) = (1-\lambda)^2\text{,}\) so the only eigenvalue is \(\lambda = 1\) with algebraic multiplicity 2.
    🔗
    To find the eigenspace, we solve \((C - I)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \implies x_2 = 0. \end{equation*}
    The eigenspace is
    \begin{equation*} H_1 = \left\{ t \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \;\middle|\; t \in \R \right\}, \end{equation*}
    so the eigenspace provides only one linearly independent eigenvector. The geometric multiplicity of \(\lambda = 1\) is 1, which is strictly less than the algebraic multiplicity 2. Therefore \(C\) is not diagonalizable.
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 1.3.27. Diagonalizability of Symmetric Matrices.

    Let \(A\) be a symmetric \(n \times n\) matrix (i.e. \(A^\top = A\)). Then:
    🔗
    • \(A\) is always diagonalizable.
      🔗
      🔗
    • There exist \(n\) pairwise orthogonal eigenvectors of \(A\text{.}\) In particular, \(A\) can be diagonalized by an orthogonal matrix \(P\text{,}\) meaning \(P^{-1} = P^\top\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    This theorem is especially important for quadratic forms (the next subsection) and for applications in mechanics, where the stress and inertia tensors are represented by symmetric matrices.
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.3 Quadratic Forms

    A quadratic form is a function that maps a vector to a real number using only squared and mixed products of its components — no linear or constant terms appear. Quadratic forms arise naturally in many contexts: the kinetic energy of a mechanical system, the elastic energy stored in a deformed structure, and the local behaviour of a differentiable function near a critical point are all described by quadratic forms. The key tool for analysing them is the diagonalization of the underlying symmetric matrix.
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.28. Quadratic Form.

    A quadratic form is a map
    \begin{equation*} q \colon \R^n \to \R, \qquad \mathbf{v} \mapsto \mathbf{v}^\top A \mathbf{v}, \end{equation*}
    where \(A\) is a symmetric \(n \times n\) matrix. The matrix \(A\) is called the matrix of the quadratic form.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.3.29.

    Every quadratic form can be uniquely represented by a symmetric matrix. If a non-symmetric matrix \(M\) is given, we can replace it by the symmetric matrix
    \begin{equation*} A = \tfrac{1}{2}(M + M^\top) \end{equation*}
    without changing the quadratic form, since \(\mathbf{v}^\top M \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}^\top A \mathbf{v}\) for all \(\mathbf{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.3.30.

    In engineering, quadratic forms often measure energy. For instance, if \(\mathbf{x}\) records two small displacements in a coupled spring system, then the elastic energy can have the form \(q(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x}\text{.}\) Positive definiteness then means that every non-zero displacement stores positive energy, which is exactly what we expect from a stable configuration.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.3.31. Expanding a Quadratic Form.

    Let
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    The associated quadratic form is
    \begin{align*} q_A(\mathbf{x}) &= \begin{pmatrix} x_1 & x_2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{pmatrix}\\ &= \begin{pmatrix} x_1 & x_2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x_1 + 2x_2 \\ 2x_1 + 3x_2 \end{pmatrix}\\ &= x_1^2 + 4x_1 x_2 + 3x_2^2. \end{align*}
    Observe that the coefficient of the mixed term \(x_1 x_2\) is \(a_{12} + a_{21} = 2 + 2 = 4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.32. Definiteness of a Quadratic Form.

    Let \(A\) be a symmetric \(n \times n\) matrix with associated quadratic form \(q(\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{v}^\top A \mathbf{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    • \(A\) is positive definite if \(q(\mathbf{v}) \gt 0\) for all \(\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(A\) is positive semidefinite if \(q(\mathbf{v}) \geq 0\) for all \(\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(A\) is negative definite if \(q(\mathbf{v}) \lt 0\) for all \(\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(A\) is negative semidefinite if \(q(\mathbf{v}) \leq 0\) for all \(\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(A\) is indefinite if \(q\) takes both positive and negative values.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.3.33. Testing Definiteness by Inspection.

    We test the definiteness of three quadratic forms.
    🔗
    (a) \(q_A(\mathbf{x}) = x_1^2 + 4x_1 x_2 + 3x_2^2\text{.}\) We evaluate at two points:
    \begin{equation*} q_A(-1, -2) = 1 + 8 + 12 = 21 \gt 0, \qquad q_A(-3, 2) = 9 - 24 + 12 = -3 \lt 0. \end{equation*}
    Since \(q_A\) takes both positive and negative values, it is indefinite.
    🔗
    (b) \(q_B(\mathbf{x}) = 3x_1^2 + 7x_2^2\text{.}\) Since \(x_1^2, x_2^2 \geq 0\text{,}\) the sum is always non-negative. It is zero only when \(x_1 = x_2 = 0\text{.}\) Therefore \(q_B\) is positive definite.
    🔗
    (c) \(q_D(\mathbf{x}) = x_1^2\text{.}\) Here \(q_D(0, 1) = 0\) even though \((0, 1)^\top \neq \mathbf{0}\text{.}\) Therefore \(q_D\) is positive semidefinite.
    🔗
    🔗
    Testing definiteness by trying specific values (as above) is fine for disproving positive definiteness, but not for proving it. The following two theorems give systematic criteria.
    🔗

    Example 1.3.34. Diagonal Quadratic Forms as Model Cases.

    For a diagonal matrix \(D=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,d_2)\text{,}\) the quadratic form is
    \begin{equation*} q_D(\mathbf{x})=d_1x_1^2+d_2x_2^2. \end{equation*}
    In this case, the sign pattern of the coefficients already reveals the geometry.
    🔗
    (a) For \(q_1(x_1,x_2)=4x_1^2+x_2^2\text{,}\) both coefficients are positive, so \(q_1(\mathbf{x})\gt 0\) for every non-zero \(\mathbf{x}\text{.}\) The level line \(q_1(\mathbf{x})=1\) is an ellipse.
    🔗
    (b) For \(q_2(x_1,x_2)=4x_1^2-x_2^2\text{,}\) one coefficient is positive and one is negative. Hence the form is indefinite. The level line \(q_2(\mathbf{x})=1\) is a hyperbola.
    🔗
    (c) For \(q_3(x_1,x_2)=x_1^2\text{,}\) we have \(q_3(\mathbf{x})\geq 0\) for all \(\mathbf{x}\text{,}\) but \(q_3(0,1)=0\text{.}\) So the form is positive semidefinite. The level set \(q_3(\mathbf{x})=1\) consists of two vertical lines.
    🔗
    These diagonal examples motivate the general criterion below: once a symmetric matrix is orthogonally diagonalized, its quadratic form reduces to exactly this kind of expression.
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 1.3.35. Definiteness Criterion via Eigenvalues.

    Let \(A\) be a symmetric \(n \times n\) matrix with eigenvalues \(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n\) (all real, by Theorem 1.3.27).
    🔗
    • \(A\) is positive definite (resp. semidefinite) if and only if all eigenvalues satisfy \(\lambda_i \gt 0\) (resp. \(\lambda_i \geq 0\)).
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(A\) is negative definite (resp. semidefinite) if and only if all eigenvalues satisfy \(\lambda_i \lt 0\) (resp. \(\lambda_i \leq 0\)).
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(A\) is indefinite if and only if there exist both a positive and a negative eigenvalue.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    By Theorem 1.3.27, there is an orthogonal matrix \(P\) and a diagonal matrix \(D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)\) such that \(A=PDP^\top\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now take any vector \(\mathbf{x}\) and write it in the form \(\mathbf{x}=P\mathbf{y}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x} = (P\mathbf{y})^\top A(P\mathbf{y}) = \mathbf{y}^\top(P^\top A P)\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{y}^\top D\mathbf{y} = \lambda_1 y_1^2+\cdots+\lambda_n y_n^2. \end{equation*}
    So the sign of the quadratic form is determined entirely by the signs of the eigenvalues.
    🔗
    If all eigenvalues are positive, then every term on the right-hand side is non-negative and at least one is positive whenever \(\mathbf{y}\neq\mathbf{0}\text{,}\) so the quadratic form is positive definite. The semidefinite, negative definite, and negative semidefinite cases are analogous. If there are both positive and negative eigenvalues, then choosing \(\mathbf{y}\) along the corresponding coordinate axes produces both positive and negative values, so the form is indefinite.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.3.36. Positive Definiteness from Eigenvalues.

    Consider
    \begin{equation*} A=\begin{pmatrix}4 & 1\\ 1 & 4\end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Its characteristic polynomial is
    \begin{equation*} p_A(\lambda) = \det\begin{pmatrix}4-\lambda & 1\\ 1 & 4-\lambda\end{pmatrix} = (4-\lambda)^2-1 = (\lambda-5)(\lambda-3). \end{equation*}
    So the eigenvalues are \(5\) and \(3\text{,}\) both positive. By the eigenvalue criterion, \(A\) is positive definite.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.3.37. Indefiniteness from Mixed Eigenvalue Signs.

    Consider
    \begin{equation*} B=\begin{pmatrix}1 & 2\\ 2 & 1\end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    We compute
    \begin{equation*} p_B(\lambda) = \det\begin{pmatrix}1-\lambda & 2\\ 2 & 1-\lambda\end{pmatrix} = (1-\lambda)^2-4 = (\lambda-3)(\lambda+1). \end{equation*}
    Thus the eigenvalues are \(3\) and \(-1\text{.}\) Since there is one positive and one negative eigenvalue, the matrix \(B\) is indefinite.
    🔗
    This agrees with direct values of the quadratic form:
    \begin{equation*} q_B(1,1)=6\gt 0, \qquad q_B(1,-1)=-2\lt 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 1.3.38. Sylvester’s Criterion.

    Let \(A\) be a symmetric \(n \times n\) matrix. Denote by \(A_i\) the \(i \times i\) leading principal submatrix (the submatrix formed by the first \(i\) rows and columns of \(A\)).
    🔗
    • \(A\) is positive definite if and only if
      \begin{equation*} \det(A_i) \gt 0 \quad \text{for all } i \in \{1, \ldots, n\}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(A\) is negative definite if and only if
      \begin{equation*} (-1)^i \det(A_i) \gt 0 \quad \text{for all } i \in \{1, \ldots, n\}. \end{equation*}
      In other words, the leading principal minors alternate in sign: \(\det(A_1) \lt 0\text{,}\) \(\det(A_2) \gt 0\text{,}\) \(\det(A_3) \lt 0\text{,}\) and so on.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.3.39. Positive Definiteness via Sylvester’s Criterion.

    Consider
    \begin{equation*} A=\begin{pmatrix}2 & 1\\ 1 & 3\end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    The leading principal submatrices are
    \begin{equation*} A_1=\begin{pmatrix}2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad A_2=A. \end{equation*}
    Their determinants are
    \begin{equation*} \det(A_1)=2\gt 0, \qquad \det(A_2)=2\cdot 3-1\cdot 1=5\gt 0. \end{equation*}
    Since all leading principal minors are positive, \(A\) is positive definite.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.3.40. Negative Definiteness via Sylvester’s Criterion.

    Consider
    \begin{equation*} B=\begin{pmatrix}-2 & 1\\ 1 & -3\end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    We compute
    \begin{equation*} \det(B_1)=-2\lt 0, \qquad \det(B)=(-2)(-3)-1=5\gt 0. \end{equation*}
    The leading principal minors alternate in sign, so Sylvester’s criterion shows that \(B\) is negative definite.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.3.41.

    In practice, Sylvester’s criterion is most useful when the eigenvalues are inconvenient to compute exactly. For a symmetric matrix, it gives a fast test for positive or negative definiteness using only determinants of smaller submatrices.

    Remark 1.3.42. Connection to Local Extrema.

    Quadratic forms play a central role in the analysis of local extrema of functions \(f \colon \R^n \to \R\text{.}\) Near a critical point \(\mathbf{x}_0\) (where \(\operatorname{grad} f(\mathbf{x}_0) = \mathbf{0}\)), the Taylor expansion gives
    \begin{equation*} f(\mathbf{x}) \approx f(\mathbf{x}_0) + \tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_0)^\top \operatorname{Hess} f(\mathbf{x}_0) (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_0). \end{equation*}
    The second-order term is a quadratic form with the Hessian matrix \(\operatorname{Hess} f(\mathbf{x}_0)\text{:}\)
    🔗
    • If \(\operatorname{Hess} f(\mathbf{x}_0)\) is positive definite, then \(\mathbf{x}_0\) is a strict local minimum.
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(\operatorname{Hess} f(\mathbf{x}_0)\) is negative definite, then \(\mathbf{x}_0\) is a strict local maximum.
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(\operatorname{Hess} f(\mathbf{x}_0)\) is indefinite, then \(\mathbf{x}_0\) is a saddle point.
      🔗
      🔗
    This connection is developed in detail in Section 2.2 (Error Propagation, Taylor Expansion, and Extrema).
    🔗

    Remark 1.3.43. Principal Axes and the Geometry of Quadratic Forms.

    Since the matrix \(A\) of a quadratic form is symmetric, it can be diagonalized by an orthogonal matrix: \(A = PDP^\top\text{.}\) Here we only use the basic idea of changing coordinates; a full general theory of change of basis is not needed yet. Substituting the coordinate change \(\mathbf{x} = P\mathbf{y}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} q(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}^\top A \mathbf{x} = (P\mathbf{y})^\top A (P\mathbf{y}) = \mathbf{y}^\top (P^\top A P) \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{y}^\top D \mathbf{y} = \lambda_1 y_1^2 + \cdots + \lambda_n y_n^2. \end{equation*}
    In the new coordinates \(\mathbf{y}\text{,}\) the quadratic form has no mixed terms. The columns of \(P\) (the eigenvectors of \(A\)) define the principal axes of the quadratic form.
    🔗
    Geometrically, the level sets \(\{\mathbf{x} \mid q(\mathbf{x}) = c\}\) are ellipses (positive definite case), hyperbolas (indefinite case), or degenerate shapes in between. The principal axes are exactly the axes of symmetry of these level curves.

    Exercises Check Your Understanding

    1. Expand a Quadratic Form.
    Let
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Write out the quadratic form \(q_A(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}^\top A \mathbf{x}\) explicitly as a polynomial in \(x_1, x_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    • \(2x_1^2- x_1x_2 + 3x_2^2\)
    • Incorrect. The mixed term receives contributions from both off-diagonal entries.
    • \(2x_1^2-2x_1x_2+3x_2^2\)
    • Correct. The mixed coefficient is \(a_{12}+a_{21}=-1+(-1)=-2\text{.}\)
    • \(2x_1^2+x_1x_2+3x_2^2\)
    • Incorrect. The sign of the mixed term is negative, not positive.
    • \(2x_1^2-2x_2^2+3x_1x_2\)
    • Incorrect. The squared terms and mixed term have been combined incorrectly.
    🔗
    2. Classify by Eigenvalues.
    Determine the eigenvalues of
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix} \end{equation*}
    and use them to decide whether \(A\) is positive definite, negative definite, or indefinite.
    🔗
    • Negative definite
    • Incorrect. Both eigenvalues are positive, not negative.
    • Positive definite
    • Correct. The eigenvalues are \(4\) and \(2\text{,}\) so both are positive.
    • Indefinite
    • Incorrect. An indefinite matrix needs both a positive and a negative eigenvalue.
    • Negative semidefinite
    • Incorrect. The matrix has strictly positive eigenvalues.
    🔗
    3. Sylvester’s Criterion for a \(3\times 3\) Matrix.
    Apply Sylvester’s criterion to determine whether
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix} \end{equation*}
    is positive definite.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Compute \(\det(A_1)\text{,}\) \(\det(A_2)\text{,}\) and \(\det(A_3)\) in turn.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The leading principal submatrices are
    \begin{equation*} A_1=\begin{pmatrix}2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad A_2=\begin{pmatrix}2 & 1\\ 1 & 3\end{pmatrix}, \qquad A_3=A. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Their determinants are
    \begin{equation*} \det(A_1)=2, \qquad \det(A_2)=2\cdot 3-1\cdot 1=5, \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} \det(A_3) = 2\det\begin{pmatrix}3 & 1\\ 1 & 4\end{pmatrix} -1\det\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1\\ 0 & 4\end{pmatrix} =2(12-1)-4 =18. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    All three leading principal minors are positive. By Sylvester’s criterion, the matrix is positive definite.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    4. Classify a Critical Point.
    The function \(f \colon \R^2 \to \R\) has a critical point at the origin with Hessian
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{Hess} f(\mathbf{0}) = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & -2 \\ -2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Is the origin a local minimum, a local maximum, or a saddle point?
    🔗
    Hint.
    Use Sylvester’s criterion or compute the eigenvalues of the Hessian.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let
    \begin{equation*} H=\begin{pmatrix}4 & -2\\ -2 & 1\end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    We apply Sylvester’s criterion. The first leading principal minor is \(\det(H_1)=4\gt 0\text{,}\) while
    \begin{equation*} \det(H)=4\cdot 1-(-2)(-2)=4-4=0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    So \(H\) is not positive definite and not negative definite. In fact, it is positive semidefinite. Therefore the second-derivative test is inconclusive here: from the Hessian alone we cannot decide whether the origin is a local minimum, a local maximum, or a saddle point.
