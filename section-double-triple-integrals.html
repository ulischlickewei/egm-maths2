    Section 4.1 Double and Triple Integrals

    This section extends integration from functions of one variable to functions of two and three variables. A double integral over a planar domain computes volumes, areas, geometric centres, and physical quantities such as charge or mass distributed over a surface. A triple integral over a three-dimensional body similarly yields volumes, centres of gravity, and moments of inertia. Both are defined as limits of Riemann sums, and both are evaluated in practice by reducing them to successive one-variable integrals — an iterated integration procedure that depends on choosing suitable coordinate systems and domain descriptions.
    🔗
    We treat double integrals first: we define them via Riemann sums, identify their main applications, and derive evaluation formulas in Cartesian and polar coordinates. Triple integrals then follow the same pattern, with Cartesian and cylindrical coordinate formulas for rotationally symmetric domains.
    🔗

    Subsection 4.1.1 Double Integrals

    The definite integral of a single-variable function measures the signed area enclosed between the graph and the horizontal axis. We now generalise this idea to functions of two variables: instead of an area, the integral will measure a volume, and instead of an interval, the domain of integration is a planar region. The construction follows the same Riemann-sum logic — approximate by simple pieces, then pass to the limit.
    🔗

    Remark 4.1.1. Review: Univariate Integrals as Riemann Sums.

    For a continuous function \(f\colon [a,b] \to \R\text{,}\) the definite integral
    \begin{equation*} \int_a^b f(x) \, dx \end{equation*}
    measures the signed area enclosed between the graph of \(f\) and the \(x\)-axis. It is defined as the limit of Riemann sums: subdivide \([a,b]\) into \(n\) sub-intervals of equal length \(\Delta x_i = \tfrac{b-a}{n}\text{,}\) choose a representative point \(x_i\) in each sub-interval, and sum the rectangle areas:
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{i=1}^n f(x_i)\,\Delta x_i \;\xrightarrow{\;n \to \infty\;}\; \int_a^b f(x) \, dx. \end{equation*}
    Each term \(f(x_i)\,\Delta x_i\) is the area of a thin rectangle of height \(f(x_i)\) and width \(\Delta x_i\text{.}\) As the subdivision is refined, the approximation converges to the exact integral.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 4.1.3. Volume Between a Bivariate Function and the \(xy\)-Plane.

    Now let \(f\colon D_f \to \R\) be a continuous function of two variables, and let \(A \subset D_f\) be a bounded planar domain. We want to compute the volume of the solid region lying between the surface \(z = f(x,y)\) and the \(xy\)-plane above \(A\text{.}\) This volume is called the double integral:
    \begin{equation*} \iint\limits_A f(x,y) \, dA, \end{equation*}
    where \(A\) is the domain of integration and \(f(x,y)\) is the integrand.
    🔗
    The construction mirrors the univariate case. Subdivide \(A\) into \(n\) small sub-domains with areas \(\Delta A_1, \ldots, \Delta A_n\text{.}\) Over each sub-domain \(A_k\text{,}\) approximate the solid by a vertical cylinder with a flat top: choose any point \((x_k, y_k) \in A_k\) and form the approximate volume
    \begin{equation*} \Delta V_k = f(x_k, y_k)\cdot\Delta A_k. \end{equation*}
    Summing over all sub-domains gives the Riemann sum approximation:
    \begin{equation*} \iint\limits_A f(x,y)\,dA \;\approx\; \sum_{k=1}^n f(x_k,y_k)\cdot\Delta A_k. \end{equation*}
    Refining the subdivision indefinitely (so that each \(\Delta A_k \to 0\)) makes this sum converge to the exact double integral.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 4.1.4. Double Integral in \(\R^2\).

    Let \(A \subset \R^2\) be a bounded domain and \(f\colon A \to \R\) a continuous function. For sub-domain areas \(\Delta A_k\) and representative points \((x_k, y_k) \in A_k\) as described above, the double integral of \(f\) over \(A\) is defined as:
    \begin{equation*} \iint\limits_A f(x,y)\,dA \;=\; \lim_{\substack{n \to \infty \\ \Delta A_k \to 0}} \sum_{k=1}^n f(x_k,y_k)\,\Delta A_k. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 4.1.5.

    This limit does not exist for all functions and all domains. In this course we work exclusively with continuous functions on bounded domains for which the double integral is well-defined.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 4.1.6. Three Basic Applications of Double Integrals.

    We now start a new line of thought. After defining the double integral as a Riemann-sum limit, we use the same idea in three concrete applications: computing the area of a planar domain, locating its geometric center, and accumulating a variable surface density such as electric charge. In each case, the double integral adds up a local contribution over all small area elements of the domain.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 4.1.7. Area of a Planar Domain.

    The area \(F(A)\) of a planar domain \(A \subseteq \R^2\) is obtained by integrating the constant function \(1\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} F(A) = \iint\limits_A 1\,dA. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 4.1.9. Geometric Center of a Planar Domain.

    Integrating the coordinate functions \(x\) and \(y\) over \(A\) gives the total coordinate moments of the domain. Dividing these moments by the total area \(F(A)\) yields the average coordinates of all area elements, that is, the coordinates of the geometric center (centroid) of \(A\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} x_C = \frac{1}{F(A)}\iint\limits_A x\,dA, \qquad y_C = \frac{1}{F(A)}\iint\limits_A y\,dA. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.1.11. Surface Charge Density.

    In physics, many quantities are specified as local densities that only yield a global value after integration. For a charged conductive plane surface \(A\text{,}\) the distribution of electric charge is described by the charge density \(\sigma(x,y)\text{,}\) where \(\sigma(x,y)\) is the charge per unit area at the point \((x,y) \in A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    To find the total charge \(q\) on the entire surface, one integrates \(\sigma\) over \(A\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} q = \iint\limits_A \sigma(x,y)\,dA. \end{equation*}
    We will return to this example in Example 4.1.27 once we have the tools to evaluate the integral explicitly.
    🔗
    🔗

    Exercises Check Your Understanding

    1. Meaning of \(\iint_D 1\,dA\).
    What does the double integral
    \begin{equation*} \iint_D 1\,dA \end{equation*}
    represent?
    🔗
    • The area of the domain \(D\)
    • Correct. Integrating the constant density \(1\) over \(D\) gives its area.
    • The perimeter of \(D\)
    • Incorrect. Perimeters are line-integral quantities, not double integrals.
    • The geometric center of \(D\)
    • Incorrect. A centroid needs moment integrals as well.
    • The slope of the boundary of \(D\)
    • Incorrect. A double integral does not describe boundary slopes.
    🔗
    2. Compute a Simple Double Integral.
    Compute
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^1\int_0^2 (x+y)\,dy\,dx. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    First integrate with respect to \(y\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^2 (x+y)\,dy = \left[xy+\frac{y^2}{2}\right]_0^2 = 2x+2. \end{equation*}
    Then integrate with respect to \(x\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^1 (2x+2)\,dx = \left[x^2+2x\right]_0^1 = 3. \end{equation*}

    Subsection 4.1.2 Evaluating Double Integrals

    A double integral over an arbitrary planar domain is difficult to evaluate directly from its definition. We therefore restrict to a family of domains — called normal domains — for which the double integral reduces to two successive one-variable integrals. This section develops the Cartesian (rectangular) and polar evaluation formulas and illustrates them with worked examples.
    🔗

    Definition 4.1.12. Normal Domains in \(\R^2\).

    A normal domain is a bounded planar region described by a pair of inequalities of one of the following two types.
    🔗
    • Type I (vertical slices): the \(x\)-boundaries are constant, and the \(y\)-boundaries depend on \(x\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} a \leq x \leq b, \qquad y_l(x) \leq y \leq y_u(x). \end{equation*}
      Here the subscripts \(l\) and \(u\) stand for lower and upper.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Type II (horizontal slices): the \(y\)-boundaries are constant, and the \(x\)-boundaries depend on \(y\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} a \leq y \leq b, \qquad g_l(y) \leq x \leq g_r(y). \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.1.14. Normal Domain Representations.

    1. Express the disk of radius 2 centered at \((1,0)\) as a normal domain of Type I.
      🔗
      The boundary circle satisfies \((x-1)^2 + y^2 = 4\text{.}\) Solving for \(y\) gives the upper and lower boundary curves:
      \begin{equation*} y = \pm\sqrt{4-(x-1)^2}. \end{equation*}
      The \(x\)-extremes of the disk are \(x = -1\) and \(x = 3\text{.}\) Therefore the domain is:
      \begin{align*} -1 \amp\leq x \leq 3,\\ -\sqrt{4-(x-1)^2} \amp\leq y \leq \sqrt{4-(x-1)^2}. \end{align*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Express the region \(A\) between the parabolas \(y = 2 - 3x^2\) and \(y = -x^2\) as a normal domain.
      🔗
      First find the intersection points:
      \begin{equation*} 2 - 3x^2 = -x^2 \;\Rightarrow\; 2 = 2x^2 \;\Rightarrow\; x = \pm 1. \end{equation*}
      For \(x \in [-1,1]\) the parabola \(y = 2-3x^2\) lies above \(y = -x^2\text{.}\) The normal domain is:
      \begin{align*} -1 \amp\leq x \leq 1,\\ -x^2 \amp\leq y \leq 2 - 3x^2. \end{align*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Remark 4.1.15. Deriving the Iterated Integral (Type I).

    For a Type I domain \(a \leq x \leq b\text{,}\) \(y_l(x) \leq y \leq y_u(x)\text{,}\) the Riemann sum can be organised in two stages:
    🔗
    1. Inner sum (fix \(x\text{,}\) sum in \(y\)). Subdivide \(A\) into vertical strips of width \(\Delta x_j\text{.}\) Within the \(j\)-th strip, subdivide further into rectangles of area \(\Delta A_{jk} = \Delta x_j \,\Delta y_k\text{.}\) The representative point \((x_j,y_k)\) is chosen inside the corresponding rectangle, so in particular \(y_l(x_j)\leq y_k\leq y_u(x_j)\text{.}\) Summing over all rectangles in this strip approximates the volume of the strip:
      \begin{equation*} V_j \approx \sum_k f(x_j, y_k)\,\Delta y_k\cdot\Delta x_j. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Outer sum (sum in \(x\)). Summing over all strips:
      \begin{equation*} \sum_j V_j \;\to\; \int_a^b \!\left(\int_{y_l(x)}^{y_u(x)} f(x,y)\,dy\right)dx. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    The inner integral (in \(y\)) is computed first, treating \(x\) as a constant. Its result depends only on \(x\) and is then integrated in the outer integral.
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 4.1.17. Double Integral in Cartesian Coordinates.

    • For a Type I normal domain (\(a \leq x \leq b\text{,}\) \(y_l(x) \leq y \leq y_u(x)\)):
      \begin{equation*} \iint\limits_A f(x,y)\,dA = \int_a^b \int_{y_l(x)}^{y_u(x)} f(x,y)\,dy\,dx. \end{equation*}
      The inner integral (variable \(y\)) is evaluated first, with \(x\) treated as a parameter.
      🔗
      🔗
    • For a Type II normal domain (\(a \leq y \leq b\text{,}\) \(g_l(y) \leq x \leq g_r(y)\)):
      \begin{equation*} \iint\limits_A f(x,y)\,dA = \int_a^b \int_{g_l(y)}^{g_r(y)} f(x,y)\,dx\,dy. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    If \(f\) is continuous on a rectangular domain \([a,b] \times [c,d]\text{,}\) the order of integration may be reversed without changing the result:
    \begin{equation*} \int_a^b \int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy\,dx = \int_c^d \int_a^b f(x,y)\,dx\,dy. \end{equation*}
    This is the simplest form of Fubini’s theorem.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.1.18. Double Integral over a Triangular Domain.

    Compute
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^1 \int_0^{1-x} (2xy - x^2 - y^2)\,dy\,dx. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The domain is the triangle with vertices \((0,0)\text{,}\) \((1,0)\text{,}\) and \((0,1)\text{,}\) described as a Type I normal domain with \(0 \leq x \leq 1\) and \(0 \leq y \leq 1 - x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Inner integral (in \(y\)):
    \begin{align*} \int_0^{1-x}(2xy - x^2 - y^2)\,dy \amp= \left[xy^2 - x^2 y - \tfrac{1}{3}y^3\right]_{y=0}^{y=1-x}\\ \amp= x(1-x)^2 - x^2(1-x) - \tfrac{1}{3}(1-x)^3. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Expanding and collecting:
    \begin{align*} \amp= x(1 - 2x + x^2) - x^2 + x^3 - \tfrac{1}{3}(1 - 3x + 3x^2 - x^3)\\ \amp= x - 2x^2 + x^3 - x^2 + x^3 - \tfrac{1}{3} + x - x^2 + \tfrac{1}{3}x^3\\ \amp= \tfrac{7}{3}x^3 - 4x^2 + 2x - \tfrac{1}{3}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Outer integral (in \(x\)):
    \begin{align*} \int_0^1\!\left(\tfrac{7}{3}x^3 - 4x^2 + 2x - \tfrac{1}{3}\right)dx \amp= \left[\tfrac{7}{12}x^4 - \tfrac{4}{3}x^3 + x^2 - \tfrac{1}{3}x\right]_0^1\\ \amp= \tfrac{7}{12} - \tfrac{16}{12} + \tfrac{12}{12} - \tfrac{4}{12} = -\tfrac{1}{12}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.1.20. Geometric Center of a Parabolic Region.

    Find the geometric center of the region \(A\) bounded by \(y = 2 - 3x^2\) and \(y = -x^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    From Example 4.1.14, the domain is \(-1 \leq x \leq 1\text{,}\) \(-x^2 \leq y \leq 2 - 3x^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    By symmetry, the centroid lies on the \(y\)-axis, so \(x_C = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Area:
    \begin{align*} F(A) \amp= \int_{-1}^1 \int_{-x^2}^{2-3x^2} 1\,dy\,dx = \int_{-1}^1 \bigl[(2-3x^2) - (-x^2)\bigr]\,dx\\ \amp= \int_{-1}^1 (2 - 2x^2)\,dx = \left[2x - \tfrac{2}{3}x^3\right]_{-1}^1 = \tfrac{8}{3}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    \(y\)-coordinate of the centroid:
    \begin{align*} y_C \amp= \frac{1}{F(A)}\int_{-1}^1\int_{-x^2}^{2-3x^2} y\,dy\,dx = \frac{3}{8}\int_{-1}^1 \left[\frac{y^2}{2}\right]_{y=-x^2}^{y=2-3x^2}dx\\ \amp= \frac{3}{8}\int_{-1}^1 \frac{(2-3x^2)^2 - x^4}{2}\,dx = \frac{3}{8}\int_{-1}^1 \left(2 - 6x^2 + 4x^4\right)\,dx\\ \amp= \frac{3}{4}\int_0^1 \left(2 - 6x^2 + 4x^4\right)\,dx = \frac{3}{4}\left[2x - 2x^3 + \frac{4}{5}x^5\right]_0^1 = \frac{3}{5}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    The geometric center is \(\bigl(0,\,\tfrac{3}{5}\bigr)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 4.1.22. Domains in Polar Coordinates.

    For domains with circular or radial symmetry it is convenient to use polar coordinates \((r, \varphi)\text{,}\) where \(x = r\cos\varphi\) and \(y = r\sin\varphi\text{.}\) A polar normal domain is described by:
    \begin{equation*} r_i(\varphi) \leq r \leq r_a(\varphi), \qquad \varphi_1 \leq \varphi \leq \varphi_2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The key point is that the area element in polar coordinates is not simply \(dr\,d\varphi\text{.}\) A small polar patch bounded by radii \(r\) and \(r + dr\) and angles \(\varphi\) and \(\varphi + d\varphi\) has area \(dA = r\,dr\,d\varphi\) (the arc length \(r\,d\varphi\) times the radial thickness \(dr\)). The extra factor \(r\) — called the Jacobian — must always appear when changing to polar coordinates.
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 4.1.24. Double Integrals in Polar Coordinates.

    For a polar normal domain \(r_i(\varphi) \leq r \leq r_a(\varphi)\text{,}\) \(\varphi_1 \leq \varphi \leq \varphi_2\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \iint\limits_A f(x,y)\,dA = \int_{\varphi_1}^{\varphi_2} \int_{r_i(\varphi)}^{r_a(\varphi)} f\bigl(r\cos\varphi,\,r\sin\varphi\bigr)\cdot r\,dr\,d\varphi. \end{equation*}
    The factor \(r\) in the integrand is the Jacobian of the coordinate change. As in Cartesian coordinates, the inner integral is evaluated first with the outer variable held fixed.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.1.25. Area and Geometric Center of a Semi-Disk.

    Find the area and geometric center of the upper half-disk of radius \(R\) (the part of the disk \(x^2 + y^2 \leq R^2\) with \(y \geq 0\)).
    🔗
    In polar coordinates the domain is \(0 \leq r \leq R\text{,}\) \(0 \leq \varphi \leq \pi\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Area:
    \begin{align*} F(D) \amp= \int_0^{\pi}\int_0^R r\,dr\,d\varphi = \int_0^{\pi}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}r^2\right]_0^R d\varphi = \tfrac{1}{2}R^2\pi. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Geometric center: By symmetry, \(x_C = 0\text{.}\) For \(y_C\text{,}\) note that \(y = r\sin\varphi\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} \iint_D y\,dA \amp= \int_0^{\pi}\int_0^R r\sin\varphi\cdot r\,dr\,d\varphi = \int_0^{\pi}\sin\varphi\,d\varphi\cdot\int_0^R r^2\,dr\\ \amp= \bigl[-\cos\varphi\bigr]_0^{\pi}\cdot\tfrac{R^3}{3} = 2\cdot\tfrac{R^3}{3} = \tfrac{2R^3}{3}. \end{align*}
    Therefore
    \begin{equation*} y_C = \frac{1}{F(D)}\iint_D y\,dA = \frac{1}{\pi R^2/2}\cdot\frac{2R^3}{3} = \frac{4R}{3\pi}. \end{equation*}
    The geometric center is at \(\bigl(0,\,\tfrac{4R}{3\pi}\bigr)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.1.27. Surface Charge via Polar Coordinates.

    A point electric charge \(q\) is located at height \(h\) above the origin of a grounded conducting plane (the \(xy\)-plane). The method of images replaces the grounded plane by a mirror charge chosen so that the plane has zero electric potential. For this setup, the induced surface charge density is
    \begin{equation*} \sigma(x,y) = -\frac{hq}{2\pi}\cdot \frac{1}{\bigl(x^2+y^2+h^2\bigr)^{3/2}}. \end{equation*}
    Compute the total induced charge on the disk of radius \(R\) centered at the origin.
    🔗
    Solution.
    In polar coordinates the disk is \(0 \leq r \leq R\text{,}\) \(0 \leq \varphi \leq 2\pi\text{,}\) and \(x^2 + y^2 = r^2\text{.}\)
    \begin{align*} q_D \amp= \iint_D \sigma\,dA = -\frac{hq}{2\pi} \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^R \frac{r}{\bigl(r^2+h^2\bigr)^{3/2}}\,dr\,d\varphi\\ \amp= -hq\int_0^R \frac{r}{\bigl(r^2+h^2\bigr)^{3/2}}\,dr. \end{align*}
    Using the substitution \(u = r^2 + h^2\text{,}\) \(du = 2r\,dr\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} \int_0^R \frac{r\,dr}{(r^2+h^2)^{3/2}} \amp= \frac{1}{2}\int_{h^2}^{R^2+h^2} u^{-3/2}\,du = \frac{1}{2}\left[-\frac{2}{\sqrt{u}}\right]_{h^2}^{R^2+h^2}\\ \amp= \frac{1}{h} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{R^2+h^2}}. \end{align*}
    Therefore
    \begin{equation*} q_D = -hq\left(\frac{1}{h} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{R^2+h^2}}\right) = q\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + R^2/h^2}} - 1\right). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.1.29. Area of an Annular Sector.

    Sketch the domain \(D = \{(r,\varphi) \mid 1 \leq r \leq 2,\; \pi/6 \leq \varphi \leq \pi\}\) and compute its area.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The domain \(D\) is a sector of the annulus between the circles \(r = 1\) and \(r = 2\text{,}\) spanning the angle range from \(\pi/6\) to \(\pi\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Area:
    \begin{align*} F(D) \amp= \int_{\pi/6}^{\pi}\int_1^2 r\,dr\,d\varphi = \int_{\pi/6}^{\pi}\left[\tfrac{r^2}{2}\right]_1^2 d\varphi = \int_{\pi/6}^{\pi}\tfrac{3}{2}\,d\varphi\\ \amp= \frac{3}{2}\!\left(\pi - \frac{\pi}{6}\right) = \frac{3}{2}\cdot\frac{5\pi}{6} = \frac{5\pi}{4}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Exercises Check Your Understanding

    1. Polar Setup for the Unit Disk.
    Which polar-coordinate description is correct for the unit disk?
    🔗
    • \(0\le r\le 1,\; 0\le \varphi\le 2\pi\)
    • Correct. This covers the full disk exactly once.
    • \(0\le r\le 2\pi,\; 0\le \varphi\le 1\)
    • Incorrect. The ranges for radius and angle are swapped.
    • \(-1\le r\le 1,\; 0\le \varphi\le \pi\)
    • Incorrect. Standard polar coordinates use nonnegative radii.
    • \(0\le r\le 1,\; 0\le \varphi\le \pi\)
    • Incorrect. That would cover only a half-disk.
    🔗
    2. Area of a Triangle by Iterated Integration.
    Compute
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^1\int_0^x 1\,dy\,dx. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    First integrate in \(y\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^x 1\,dy = x. \end{equation*}
    Then
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^1 x\,dx = \left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_0^1 = \frac12. \end{equation*}
    So the integral equals \(\tfrac12\text{.}\)

    Subsection 4.1.3 Triple Integrals

    The same Riemann-sum construction that defines double integrals over planar regions extends naturally to functions of three variables integrated over three-dimensional domains. A triple integral
    \begin{equation*} \iiint\limits_V f(x,y,z)\,dV \end{equation*}
    sums the values of \(f\) over an infinite number of infinitesimally small volume elements \(dV\text{.}\) When \(f = 1\text{,}\) the triple integral gives the ordinary volume of the domain. For a general integrand, it accumulates a density over the body, such as mass density or moment-of-inertia density. This is why triple integrals appear throughout physics as natural tools for computing mass, center of gravity, and moments of inertia of three-dimensional bodies.
    🔗

    Remark 4.1.31. Construction of the Triple Integral.

    Let \(f\colon V \to \R\) be a continuous function on a bounded spatial domain \(V \subset \R^3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. Subdivide \(V\) into \(n\) small sub-domains of volumes \(\Delta V_1, \ldots, \Delta V_n\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. In each sub-domain \(V_k\text{,}\) choose an arbitrary representative point \((x_k, y_k, z_k)\) and form the approximation \(f(x_k,y_k,z_k)\cdot\Delta V_k\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Sum over all sub-domains:
      \begin{equation*} \iiint\limits_V f(x,y,z)\,dV \approx \sum_{k=1}^n f(x_k,y_k,z_k)\,\Delta V_k. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    Passing to the limit as \(n \to \infty\) and each \(\Delta V_k \to 0\) gives the exact triple integral.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 4.1.32. Triple Integral.

    Let \(V \subset \R^3\) be a bounded domain and \(f\colon V \to \R\) a continuous function. The triple integral of \(f\) over \(V\) is:
    \begin{equation*} \iiint\limits_V f(x,y,z)\,dV \;=\; \lim_{\substack{n \to \infty \\ \Delta V_k \to 0}} \sum_{k=1}^n f(x_k,y_k,z_k)\,\Delta V_k. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 4.1.33. Volume as Accumulated Unit Density.

    A triple integral can be read as adding up contributions from tiny volume elements. If the integrand is the constant function \(1\text{,}\) each element contributes exactly its own volume \(dV\text{.}\) Summing all these pieces recovers the volume of the whole spatial domain.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 4.1.35. Volume of a Spatial Domain.

    The volume of a domain \(V \subseteq \R^3\) is:
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{vol}(V) = \iiint\limits_V 1\,dV. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 4.1.36. Centroid as Average Position.

    For a body of uniform density, the geometric center is the average position of all volume elements. The coordinate integrals add the \(x\)-, \(y\)-, and \(z\)-coordinates of each small piece and then divide by the total volume.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 4.1.37. Geometric Center of a Spatial Domain.

    The coordinates \((x_S, y_S, z_S)\) of the geometric center (centroid) of \(V \subseteq \R^3\) are:
    \begin{align*} x_S \amp= \frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}(V)}\iiint\limits_V x\,dV,\\ y_S \amp= \frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}(V)}\iiint\limits_V y\,dV,\\ z_S \amp= \frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}(V)}\iiint\limits_V z\,dV. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 4.1.38. Mass and Center of Gravity for Variable Density.

    If density varies from point to point, equal volume elements no longer contribute equally to the balance point. The mass integral weights each small volume element by its density \(\rho(x,y,z)\text{.}\) The center of gravity is then the density-weighted average position, so it is pulled toward regions where the body is heavier.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 4.1.39. Mass and Center of Gravity.

    For a body \(V \subseteq \R^3\) with variable mass density \(\rho(x,y,z)\) (mass per unit volume), the total mass and center of gravity are:
    \begin{align*} m \amp= \iiint\limits_V \rho(x,y,z)\,dV,\\ x_S \amp= \frac{1}{m}\iiint\limits_V x\cdot\rho(x,y,z)\,dV,\\ y_S \amp= \frac{1}{m}\iiint\limits_V y\cdot\rho(x,y,z)\,dV,\\ z_S \amp= \frac{1}{m}\iiint\limits_V z\cdot\rho(x,y,z)\,dV. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 4.1.40.

    If the density \(\rho\) is constant throughout the body, the center of gravity coincides with the geometric center. A non-uniform density shifts the center of gravity towards regions of higher density.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 4.1.41. Moments of Inertia as Weighted Squared Distance.

    Rotating a body about an axis is harder when more mass lies far away from that axis. A moment of inertia therefore weights each small mass element by the square of its perpendicular distance to the axis. For example, the distance from \((x,y,z)\) to the \(z\)-axis is \(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\text{,}\) so the corresponding squared distance is \(x^2+y^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 4.1.42. Moments of Inertia.

    For a body \(V\) with density \(\rho(x,y,z)\text{,}\) the moments of inertia with respect to the \(x\)-, \(y\)-, and \(z\)-axes are:
    \begin{align*} J_x \amp= \iiint\limits_V (y^2+z^2)\,\rho(x,y,z)\,dV,\\ J_y \amp= \iiint\limits_V (x^2+z^2)\,\rho(x,y,z)\,dV,\\ J_z \amp= \iiint\limits_V (x^2+y^2)\,\rho(x,y,z)\,dV. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.1.4 Evaluating Triple Integrals

    Triple integrals are evaluated by reducing them to three successive one-variable integrals, exactly as for double integrals. For a chosen order of integration, the domain must be expressible as a normal domain: the bounds of an inner variable may depend on the outer variables that are already fixed, but not on variables that are integrated earlier. We treat Cartesian and cylindrical coordinates in turn.
    🔗

    Remark 4.1.43. Normal Domains in \(\R^3\).

    For the integration order \(dz\,dy\,dx\text{,}\) a normal domain in three dimensions is a spatial region described by inequalities of the form:
    \begin{align*} a \amp\leq x \leq b,\\ y_l(x) \amp\leq y \leq y_u(x),\\ z_l(x,y) \amp\leq z \leq z_u(x,y). \end{align*}
    🔗
    The structure is hierarchical:
    🔗
    • The outer bounds (for \(x\)) are constants.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The middle bounds (for \(y\)) may depend on \(x\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • The inner bounds (for \(z\)) may depend on both \(x\) and \(y\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Other variable orderings, such as \(dx\,dz\,dy\text{,}\) are equally valid. The useful order is the one whose bounds describe the geometry most simply.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.1.44. Cylinder as a Normal Domain.

    Show that a solid cylinder of radius \(R\) and height \(h\text{,}\) with its base centred at the origin, is a normal domain.
    🔗
    Project the cylinder onto the \(xy\)-plane: the cross-section is the disk \(x^2 + y^2 \leq R^2\text{.}\) Describing this disk as a Type I planar normal domain gives \(-R \leq x \leq R\) and \(-\sqrt{R^2 - x^2} \leq y \leq \sqrt{R^2 - x^2}\text{.}\) The height runs from \(0\) to \(h\text{.}\) Combining:
    \begin{align*} -R \amp\leq x \leq R,\\ -\sqrt{R^2-x^2} \amp\leq y \leq \sqrt{R^2-x^2},\\ 0 \amp\leq z \leq h. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 4.1.46. Triple Integrals in Cartesian Coordinates.

    For a normal domain \(V\) with \(a \leq x \leq b\text{,}\) \(y_l(x) \leq y \leq y_u(x)\text{,}\) \(z_l(x,y) \leq z \leq z_u(x,y)\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \iiint\limits_V f(x,y,z)\,dV = \int_a^b \int_{y_l(x)}^{y_u(x)} \int_{z_l(x,y)}^{z_u(x,y)} f(x,y,z)\,dz\,dy\,dx. \end{equation*}
    The innermost integral (in \(z\)) is computed first, treating \(x\) and \(y\) as parameters. The middle integral (in \(y\)) is then computed, treating \(x\) as a parameter. The outermost integral (in \(x\)) is an ordinary definite integral.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.1.47. Triple Integral with Trigonometric Integrand.

    Let \(f(x,y,z) = yz\sin x\) and
    \begin{equation*} V = \{(x,y,z) \in \R^3 \mid 0 \leq x \leq \tfrac{\pi}{2},\; 0 \leq y \leq 1,\; y^2 \leq z \leq y\}. \end{equation*}
    Compute
    \begin{equation*} \iiint\limits_V yz\sin x\,dV. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    Note that for \(y \in [0,1]\) we have \(y^2 \leq y\text{,}\) so the \(z\)-bounds are valid.
    🔗
    Inner integral (in \(z\)):
    \begin{align*} \int_{y^2}^{y} yz\sin x\,dz \amp= y\sin x\left[\tfrac{1}{2}z^2\right]_{y^2}^{y} = \tfrac{1}{2}y\sin x\,(y^2 - y^4). \end{align*}
    🔗
    Middle integral (in \(y\)):
    \begin{align*} \int_0^1 \tfrac{1}{2}y\sin x\,(y^2 - y^4)\,dy \amp= \tfrac{\sin x}{2}\left[\tfrac{y^4}{4} - \tfrac{y^6}{6}\right]_0^1 = \tfrac{\sin x}{2}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{4} - \tfrac{1}{6}\right) = \tfrac{\sin x}{24}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Outer integral (in \(x\)):
    \begin{align*} \int_0^{\pi/2} \tfrac{\sin x}{24}\,dx \amp= \tfrac{1}{24}\left[-\cos x\right]_0^{\pi/2} = \tfrac{1}{24}(0 - (-1)) = \tfrac{1}{24}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.1.49. Triple Integral with Exponential Integrand.

    Let \(f(x,y,z) = ye^z\) and
    \begin{equation*} V = \{(x,y,z)\in\R^3 \mid 1 \leq x \leq 2,\; 0 \leq y \leq x,\; 0 \leq z \leq x-y\}. \end{equation*}
    Compute
    \begin{equation*} \iiint\limits_V ye^z\,dV. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    Inner integral (in \(z\)):
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^{x-y} ye^z\,dz = y\left[e^z\right]_0^{x-y} = y(e^{x-y} - 1). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Middle integral (in \(y\)): Split into two parts:
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^x y(e^{x-y} - 1)\,dy = \int_0^x ye^{x-y}\,dy - \int_0^x y\,dy. \end{equation*}
    For the first part, integrate by parts with \(u = y\text{,}\) \(v' = e^{x-y}\) (so \(v = -e^{x-y}\)):
    \begin{align*} \int_0^x ye^{x-y}\,dy \amp= \left[-ye^{x-y}\right]_0^x + \int_0^x e^{x-y}\,dy\\ \amp= -x + \left[-e^{x-y}\right]_0^x = -x - 1 + e^x. \end{align*}
    The second part: \(\int_0^x y\,dy = \tfrac{x^2}{2}\text{.}\) Combining:
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^x y(e^{x-y}-1)\,dy = e^x - x - 1 - \tfrac{x^2}{2}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Outer integral (in \(x\)):
    \begin{align*} \int_1^2\!\left(e^x - x - 1 - \tfrac{x^2}{2}\right)dx \amp= \left[e^x - \tfrac{x^2}{2} - x - \tfrac{x^3}{6}\right]_1^2\\ \amp= \left(e^2 - 2 - 2 - \tfrac{4}{3}\right) - \left(e - \tfrac{1}{2} - 1 - \tfrac{1}{6}\right)\\ \amp= e^2 - e - \tfrac{11}{3}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 4.1.51. Cylindrical Coordinates.

    For spatial domains with rotational symmetry about the \(z\)-axis, the computation simplifies greatly in cylindrical coordinates. A point \(P = (x,y,z)\) is described by:
    🔗
    • \((r,\varphi)\text{:}\) polar coordinates of the orthogonal projection of \(P\) into the \(xy\)-plane, with \(x = r\cos\varphi\) and \(y = r\sin\varphi\text{,}\) \(r \geq 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(z\text{:}\) the height coordinate, unchanged.
      🔗
      🔗
    The volume element in cylindrical coordinates is
    \begin{equation*} dV = r\,dr\,d\varphi\,dz. \end{equation*}
    The factor \(r\) is the Jacobian of the transformation from \((x,y,z)\) to \((r,\varphi,z)\text{,}\) and arises for the same reason as in polar coordinates for double integrals (see Remark 4.1.22).
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 4.1.53. Triple Integrals in Cylindrical Coordinates.

    For a normal domain \(V\) described in cylindrical coordinates by \(r_0 \leq r \leq r_1\text{,}\) \(f_i(r) \leq \varphi \leq f_a(r)\text{,}\) \(z_l(r,\varphi) \leq z \leq z_u(r,\varphi)\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \iiint\limits_V f(x,y,z)\,dV = \int_{r_0}^{r_1} \int_{f_i(r)}^{f_a(r)} \int_{z_l(r,\varphi)}^{z_u(r,\varphi)} f(r\cos\varphi,\,r\sin\varphi,\,z)\cdot r \,dz\,d\varphi\,dr. \end{equation*}
    The Jacobian factor \(r\) must always be included. As in Cartesian coordinates, other orders of integration are possible if they describe the same solid more simply.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.1.54. Moment of Inertia of a Solid Cylinder.

    Consider a solid cylinder with radius \(R\text{,}\) height \(h\) (centred at the origin, so \(-h/2 \leq z \leq h/2\)), and constant mass density \(\rho\text{.}\) The total mass is \(m = \rho\operatorname{vol}(Z) = \rho\pi R^2 h\text{.}\) Compute the moment of inertia \(J_z\) about the \(z\)-axis.
    🔗
    In cylindrical coordinates, the cylinder is \(0 \leq r \leq R\text{,}\) \(0 \leq \varphi \leq 2\pi\text{,}\) \(-h/2 \leq z \leq h/2\text{.}\) The squared distance from the \(z\)-axis is \(x^2 + y^2 = r^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} J_z \amp= \iiint\limits_Z (x^2+y^2)\,\rho\,dV = \rho\int_0^R\int_0^{2\pi}\int_{-h/2}^{h/2} r^2 \cdot r\,dz\,d\varphi\,dr\\ \amp= \rho\int_0^R\int_0^{2\pi} r^3 h\,d\varphi\,dr = \rho\cdot 2\pi h\int_0^R r^3\,dr = 2\pi\rho h\cdot\frac{R^4}{4} = \frac{\pi\rho h R^4}{2}. \end{align*}
    Since \(m = \rho\pi R^2 h\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} J_z = \frac{1}{2}mR^2. \end{equation*}
    This classical result states that the moment of inertia of a cylinder about its axis of symmetry equals half the product of its mass and the square of its radius.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.1.55. Geometric Center of a Half-Ball.

    Find the geometric center of the upper half-ball of radius \(R\text{:}\) \(V = \{(x,y,z) \mid x^2 + y^2 + z^2 \leq R^2,\; z \geq 0\}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    By symmetry, \(x_S = y_S = 0\text{.}\) In cylindrical coordinates, the half-ball is bounded by \(0 \leq r \leq R\text{,}\) \(0 \leq \varphi \leq 2\pi\text{,}\) \(0 \leq z \leq \sqrt{R^2 - r^2}\text{.}\) The volume is \(\operatorname{vol}(V) = \tfrac{2}{3}\pi R^3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \(z\)-coordinate of the centroid:
    \begin{align*} \iiint_V z\,dV \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^R\int_0^{\sqrt{R^2-r^2}} z\cdot r\,dz\,dr\,d\varphi\\ \amp= 2\pi\int_0^R r\left[\frac{z^2}{2}\right]_0^{\sqrt{R^2-r^2}}dr = 2\pi\int_0^R \frac{r(R^2-r^2)}{2}\,dr\\ \amp= \pi\int_0^R (R^2 r - r^3)\,dr = \pi\left[\frac{R^2 r^2}{2} - \frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^R = \pi\left(\frac{R^4}{2} - \frac{R^4}{4}\right) = \frac{\pi R^4}{4}. \end{align*}
    Therefore
    \begin{equation*} z_S = \frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}(V)}\iiint_V z\,dV = \frac{1}{\tfrac{2}{3}\pi R^3}\cdot\frac{\pi R^4}{4} = \frac{3}{8}R. \end{equation*}
    The geometric center of the half-ball is at \(\bigl(0,\,0,\,\tfrac{3}{8}R\bigr)\text{.}\)

    2. Compute a Cylindrical Triple Integral.
    Compute
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1\int_0^2 z\,r\,dz\,dr\,d\varphi. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.

    Then
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^1 2r\,dr = \left[r^2\right]_0^1 = 1. \end{equation*}
    Finally,
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^{2\pi} 1\,d\varphi = 2\pi. \end{equation*}
    Hence the value of the integral is \(2\pi\text{.}\)
