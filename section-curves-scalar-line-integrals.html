    Section 4.2 Curves and Scalar Line Integrals

    This section introduces two closely related topics. First, we develop the concept of a parametrized curve in \(\R^n\text{:}\) a path traced by a moving point, described by a vector-valued function of a single parameter. We define its trace, its tangent vector (the instantaneous velocity), and establish the differentiation rules that govern combinations of curves.
    🔗
    Second, we use these ideas to define the scalar line integral: an integral of a function along a curve. The key insight is that integrating “with respect to arc length” requires weighting by the speed of the parametrization, which ensures the result is independent of how the curve is traversed. Applications include computing arc lengths, masses of wires with variable density, and centers of gravity.
    🔗

    Subsection 4.2.1 Curves in \(\R^n\)

    Up to now, integration has concerned itself with regions in the plane or in space. This section and the next move from regions to curves: one-dimensional paths winding through two- or three-dimensional space. We begin by making the notion of a parametrized curve precise, and then develop the calculus tools — tangent vectors, velocities, and differentiation rules — that are needed to compute line integrals in Subsection 4.2.2.
    🔗

    Definition 4.2.1. Curve, Trace, Closed Curve, Differentiable Curve.

    Let \(I \subseteq \R\) be an interval.
    🔗
    A curve parametrized over \(I\) is a function
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}\colon I \to \R^n, \qquad t \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} x_1(t) \\ \vdots \\ x_n(t) \end{pmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    whose component functions \(x_1, \ldots, x_n \colon I \to \R\) are continuous. The parameter \(t\) is often thought of as time.
    🔗
    The set of all values taken by \(\mathbf{x}\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}(I) = \left\{\, \begin{pmatrix} x_1(t) \\ \vdots \\ x_n(t) \end{pmatrix} \;\middle|\; t \in I \,\right\} \subseteq \R^n, \end{equation*}
    is called the trace of \(\mathbf{x}\text{.}\) The trace is the actual geometric path in space; the parametrization is one particular way of traversing that path.
    🔗
    If \(I = [a,b]\) and \(\mathbf{x}(a) = \mathbf{x}(b)\text{,}\) the curve is closed (the path returns to its starting point). If the component functions \(x_1, \ldots, x_n\) are continuously differentiable, the curve is continuously differentiable.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.2.2. Helix.

    Sketch the trace of the curve
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}\colon [0, 4\pi] \to \R^3, \qquad t \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} \cos t \\ \sin t \\ t \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    The first two components \((\cos t, \sin t)\) trace the unit circle in the \(xy\)-plane, while the third component \(t\) increases monotonically from \(0\) to \(4\pi\text{.}\) The result is a helix that spirals upward around the \(z\)-axis, completing two full turns.

    Example 4.2.5. Intersection Curve of a Cylinder with a Plane.

    Determine a parametrization of the curve of intersection of the cylinder \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\) with the plane \(y + z = 2\text{.}\) That is, find an interval \(I \subset \R\) and a curve \(\mathbf{x}\colon I \to \R^3\) whose trace is the intersection.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The projection of the intersection onto the \(xy\)-plane is the unit circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\text{,}\) so we parametrize
    \begin{equation*} x(t) = \cos t, \qquad y(t) = \sin t, \qquad t \in [0, 2\pi]. \end{equation*}
    For the point \((\cos t, \sin t, z(t))\) to lie in the plane \(y + z = 2\text{,}\) we need
    \begin{equation*} z(t) = 2 - \sin t. \end{equation*}
    The parametrization is therefore
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}\colon [0,2\pi] \to \R^3, \qquad t \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} \cos t \\ \sin t \\ 2 - \sin t \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    The trace is an ellipse tilted in space: at \(y = 0\) the height is \(z = 2\text{,}\) while at \(y = 1\) (the highest circle point) the height is \(z = 1\text{,}\) and at \(y = -1\) it is \(z = 3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 4.2.7. Tangent Vector, Velocity Vector, Speed, Regular Curve.

    Let \(\mathbf{x}\colon I \to \R^n\) be a differentiable curve.
    🔗
    The tangent vector of \(\mathbf{x}\) at time \(t\) is the component-wise derivative
    \begin{equation*} \dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}(t) = \begin{pmatrix} \dot{x}_1(t) \\ \vdots \\ \dot{x}_n(t) \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    The derivative \(\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)\) is also called the velocity vector. Its length
    \begin{equation*} \|\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)\| = \sqrt{\dot{x}_1(t)^2 + \cdots + \dot{x}_n(t)^2} \end{equation*}
    is called the speed at time \(t\text{:}\) it measures how fast a particle traversing the curve moves at the instant \(t\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If \(\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) \neq \mathbf{0}\) for all \(t \in I\text{,}\) the curve is called regular. For a regular curve the tangent vector always points in a definite direction at every parameter value.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.2.9. Tangent Vector of a Circle.

    Compute the tangent vector of the unit circle \(\mathbf{x}\colon [0,2\pi] \to \R^2\text{,}\) \(t \mapsto (\cos t,\, \sin t)\text{,}\) and verify that it is always orthogonal to the position vector.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Differentiating component-wise:
    \begin{equation*} \dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \begin{pmatrix} -\sin t \\ \cos t \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    To check orthogonality, compute the inner product with the position vector:
    \begin{equation*} \langle \mathbf{x}(t),\, \dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) \rangle = \cos t \cdot (-\sin t) + \sin t \cdot \cos t = 0. \end{equation*}
    The tangent vector is indeed always perpendicular to the radius. The velocity is \(\|\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)\| = \sqrt{\sin^2 t + \cos^2 t} = 1\text{,}\) confirming that the unit circle is traversed at constant speed.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.2.11. Angle of Intersection of Two Curves.

    The graphs of \(f(x) = e^{x-1}\) and \(g(x) = x^2\) intersect at \((1,1)\text{.}\) Determine the angle of intersection by parametrizing the graphs as curves and computing the angle between their tangent vectors at the intersection point.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Step 1: Parametrize both graphs as curves.
    \begin{gather*} \mathbf{x}_1\colon \R \to \R^2, \quad t \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} t \\ e^{t-1} \end{pmatrix};\\ \mathbf{x}_2\colon \R \to \R^2, \quad t \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} t \\ t^2 \end{pmatrix}. \end{gather*}
    Both curves pass through \((1,1)\) at \(t = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 2: Compute the tangent vectors at \(t = 1\text{.}\)
    \begin{gather*} \dot{\mathbf{x}}_1(t) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ e^{t-1} \end{pmatrix} \;\Rightarrow\; \dot{\mathbf{x}}_1(1) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix},\\ \dot{\mathbf{x}}_2(t) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2t \end{pmatrix} \;\Rightarrow\; \dot{\mathbf{x}}_2(1) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}. \end{gather*}
    🔗
    Step 3: Compute the angle between the tangent vectors.
    \begin{gather*} \cos\alpha = \frac{\langle \dot{\mathbf{x}}_1(1),\, \dot{\mathbf{x}}_2(1) \rangle} {\|\dot{\mathbf{x}}_1(1)\|\cdot\|\dot{\mathbf{x}}_2(1)\|} = \frac{1 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 2}{\sqrt{1^2+1^2}\cdot\sqrt{1^2+2^2}} = \frac{3}{\sqrt{2}\cdot\sqrt{5}} = \frac{3}{\sqrt{10}}. \end{gather*}
    Therefore
    \begin{equation*} \alpha = \arccos\!\left(\frac{3}{\sqrt{10}}\right) \approx 18.43^\circ. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 4.2.13. Differentiation Rules for Curves.

    Let \(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2\colon I \to \R^n\) be differentiable curves, let \(a\colon I \to \R\) be a scalar function, and let \(a_1, a_2 \in \R\text{.}\) Then:
    🔗
    Linearity:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dt}\!\big(a_1 \mathbf{x}_1(t) + a_2 \mathbf{x}_2(t)\big) = a_1\,\dot{\mathbf{x}}_1(t) + a_2\,\dot{\mathbf{x}}_2(t). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Product rule (inner product):
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dt}\!\big\langle \mathbf{x}_1(t), \mathbf{x}_2(t) \big\rangle = \langle \dot{\mathbf{x}}_1(t), \mathbf{x}_2(t) \rangle + \langle \mathbf{x}_1(t), \dot{\mathbf{x}}_2(t) \rangle. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Product rule (scalar multiplication):
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dt}\!\big(a(t)\,\mathbf{x}(t)\big) = \dot{a}(t)\,\mathbf{x}(t) + a(t)\,\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Product rule (cross product, only for \(n = 3\)):
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dt}\!\big(\mathbf{x}_1(t) \times \mathbf{x}_2(t)\big) = \dot{\mathbf{x}}_1(t) \times \mathbf{x}_2(t) + \mathbf{x}_1(t) \times \dot{\mathbf{x}}_2(t). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    All rules follow from the component-wise definition of \(\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)\) and the corresponding single-variable differentiation rules applied component by component. The cross-product rule additionally uses the bilinearity and antisymmetry of the cross product.
    🔗

    Let
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos t\\ \sin t\end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    What is the speed \(\|\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)\|\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • \(0\)
    • Incorrect. The point is moving along the circle.
    • \(1\)
    • Correct. Since \(\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)=(-\sin t,\cos t)\text{,}\) its norm is always \(1\text{.}\)
    • \(\sqrt{2}\)
    • Incorrect. The two squared components add to \(1\text{,}\) not \(2\text{.}\)
    • \(t\)
    • Incorrect. The speed is constant here.

    2. Tangent Vector of a Parametrized Curve.
    For
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}t\\ t^2\end{pmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    compute the tangent vector at \(t=1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Differentiate componentwise:
    \begin{equation*} \dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}1\\ 2t\end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Therefore
    \begin{equation*} \dot{\mathbf{x}}(1)=\begin{pmatrix}1\\ 2\end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}

    Subsection 4.2.2 Line Integrals of Scalar Fields

    A single-variable integral sums a function over an interval on the number line; a double integral sums over a region in the plane. A scalar line integral sums a function along a curve in the plane or in space. Geometrically it measures the area of the curved “curtain” suspended above the curve up to the height given by the function. Physically it computes the mass of a wire whose density varies along its length, or the arc length of the curve itself.
    🔗

    Definition 4.2.14. Line Integral of a Scalar Field.

    Let \(\mathbf{x}\colon [a,b] \to \R^n\) be a continuously differentiable curve, let \(D \subseteq \R^n\) contain the trace \(C = \mathbf{x}([a,b])\text{,}\) and let \(f\colon D \to \R\) be a continuous scalar function (also called a scalar field).
    🔗
    The line integral of \(f\) along \(C\) is
    \begin{equation*} \int_C f \, ds := \int_a^b f\!\left(\mathbf{x}(t)\right) \, \|\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)\| \, dt. \end{equation*}
    Here \(ds = \|\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)\|\,dt\) is the arc length element: it converts a small time increment \(dt\) into the corresponding arc length traversed along the curve.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 4.2.15. Independence of Parametrization.

    The value of the line integral does not depend on the particular parametrization chosen for the curve. More precisely: if two continuously differentiable curves \(\mathbf{x}\colon [a,b] \to \R^n\) and \(\mathbf{y}\colon [c,d] \to \R^n\) have the same trace \(C = \mathbf{x}([a,b]) = \mathbf{y}([c,d])\text{,}\) and one is obtained from the other by a continuously differentiable reparametrization that traverses \(C\) exactly once, then
    \begin{equation*} \int_a^b f\!\left(\mathbf{x}(t)\right) \, \|\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)\| \, dt = \int_c^d f\!\left(\mathbf{y}(u)\right) \, \|\dot{\mathbf{y}}(u)\| \, du. \end{equation*}
    Reversing the direction of traversal also leaves the value unchanged, because \(ds\) measures arc length and is always nonnegative. This allows us to write the integral using the trace alone, without specifying a particular parametrization.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 4.2.16. Geometric Interpretation.

    Analogously to the single-variable integral, the scalar line integral \(\int_C f \, ds\) computes the area of the curved curtain (or fence) erected above \(C\) up to the height \(f\text{.}\) At each point of the curve the curtain has height \(f\text{,}\) and the arc length element \(ds\) weights each infinitesimal strip by how long the corresponding piece of curve is.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 4.2.18. How to Compute a Scalar Line Integral.

    To evaluate \(\int_C f \, ds\text{,}\) follow three steps:
    🔗
    Step 1: Parametrize the curve. Find a continuously differentiable parametrization \(\mathbf{x}\colon [a,b] \to \R^n\) with trace \(C\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 2: Compute the velocity.
    \begin{equation*} \|\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)\| = \sqrt{\dot{x}_1(t)^2 + \cdots + \dot{x}_n(t)^2}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Step 3: Evaluate the one-variable integral.
    \begin{equation*} \int_C f \, ds = \int_a^b f\!\left(\mathbf{x}(t)\right) \, \|\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)\| \, dt. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The arc length factor \(\|\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)\|\) compensates for the velocity at which the parameter traverses the curve, ensuring the result is independent of the chosen parametrization.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.2.19. Line Integral Along the Upper Half-Circle.

    Compute the line integral \(\displaystyle\int_C f \, ds\) of the scalar field \(f(x,y) = 2 + x^2 y\) along the upper semicircle of radius \(1\) (i.e.\ the arc with \(y \ge 0\)).
    🔗
    Solution.
    Step 1: Parametrize.
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}\colon [0,\pi] \to \R^2, \qquad t \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} \cos t \\ \sin t \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Step 2: Compute the velocity.
    \begin{equation*} \dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \begin{pmatrix} -\sin t \\ \cos t \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \|\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)\| = \sqrt{\sin^2 t + \cos^2 t} = 1. \end{equation*}
    The unit-circle parametrization has constant velocity of magnitude \(\|\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)\| = 1\) throughout.
    🔗
    Step 3: Evaluate the integral.
    \begin{align*} \int_C f \, ds \amp= \int_0^\pi f(\cos t, \sin t) \cdot 1 \, dt\\ \amp= \int_0^\pi \bigl(2 + \cos^2\!t \cdot \sin t\bigr) \, dt. \end{align*}
    The first part: \(\displaystyle\int_0^\pi 2 \, dt = 2\pi\text{.}\) The second part, using the substitution \(u = \cos t\text{,}\) \(du = -\sin t \, dt\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^\pi \cos^2\!t \cdot \sin t \, dt = \int_1^{-1} u^2 \cdot (-\,du) = \int_{-1}^1 u^2 \, du = \left[\frac{u^3}{3}\right]_{-1}^{1} = \frac{2}{3}. \end{equation*}
    Therefore
    \begin{equation*} \int_C f \, ds = 2\pi + \frac{2}{3}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 4.2.21. Arc Length of a Curve.

    The special case \(f \equiv 1\) yields the arc length: if \(C \subset \R^n\) has a continuously differentiable parametrization \(\mathbf{x}\colon [a,b] \to \R^n\) with trace \(C\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} L(C) = \int_C \, ds = \int_a^b \|\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)\| \, dt. \end{equation*}
    The arc length element \(ds = \|\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)\|\,dt\) accumulates the infinitesimal lengths of the curve.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.2.22. Arc Length with a Non-Uniform Parametrization.

    Compute the arc length of the curve
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}\colon [0,1] \to \R^2, \qquad t \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} t^2 \\ t^2 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    This parametrization traces the line segment from \((0,0)\) to \((1,1)\text{,}\) but not at constant speed.
    🔗
    Solution.
    First compute the derivative:
    \begin{equation*} \dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \begin{pmatrix} 2t \\ 2t \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \|\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)\| = \sqrt{(2t)^2 + (2t)^2} = 2\sqrt{2}\,t. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Therefore the arc length is
    \begin{equation*} L(C) = \int_0^1 \|\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)\| \, dt = \int_0^1 2\sqrt{2}\,t \, dt = 2\sqrt{2}\left[\frac{t^2}{2}\right]_0^1 = \sqrt{2}. \end{equation*}
    This agrees with the geometric length of the line segment joining \((0,0)\) and \((1,1)\text{.}\) The factor \(\|\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)\|\) corrects for the non-uniform motion along the same trace.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.2.23. Mass and Center of Gravity of a Wire.

    Line integrals of scalar fields arise naturally in physics when computing the mass and center of gravity of a thin wire.
    🔗
    Let \(\rho\colon C \to \R\) be the linear mass density of a thin wire shaped along a curve \(C \subseteq \R^3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    • The mass of the wire is
      \begin{equation*} M = \int_C \rho \, ds. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    • The coordinates \((x_S, y_S, z_S)\) of the center of gravity are
      \begin{equation*} x_S = \frac{1}{M}\int_C x\,\rho \, ds, \quad y_S = \frac{1}{M}\int_C y\,\rho \, ds, \quad z_S = \frac{1}{M}\int_C z\,\rho \, ds. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    Each formula is a line integral of the scalar field \(\rho\text{,}\) or of \(x\rho\text{,}\) \(y\rho\text{,}\) \(z\rho\text{,}\) along the curve \(C\) representing the wire. When the density is constant \(\rho \equiv \rho_0\text{,}\) the mass reduces to \(M = \rho_0 \, L(C)\text{,}\) confirming that the arc length is the correct measure of the wire’s size.
    🔗
    🔗

    Exercises Check Your Understanding

    1. Why the Speed Factor Appears.
    In the scalar line integral
    \begin{equation*} \int_C f\,ds = \int_a^b f(\mathbf{x}(t))\|\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)\|\,dt, \end{equation*}
    why does the factor \(\|\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)\|\) appear?
    🔗
    • It converts parameter change into arc length and makes the integral independent of parametrization.
    • Correct. The speed factor tells us how much curve length is traversed per unit change of the parameter.
    • It makes every line integral equal to the enclosed area.
    • Incorrect. Scalar line integrals do not generally compute enclosed areas.
    • It forces the integrand to be a vector field.
    • Incorrect. This is a scalar line integral.
    • It is needed only for closed curves.
    • Incorrect. The factor is needed for any parametrized curve.
    🔗
    2. Arc Length of a Line Segment.
    Compute
    \begin{equation*} \int_C 1\,ds \end{equation*}
    where \(C\) is the line segment from \((0,0)\) to \((3,4)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since the integrand is \(1\text{,}\) the scalar line integral equals the arc length of the segment. The distance between the endpoints is
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5. \end{equation*}
    Therefore
    \begin{equation*} \int_C 1\,ds = 5. \end{equation*}
