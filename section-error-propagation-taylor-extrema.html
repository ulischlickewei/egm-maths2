    Section 2.2 Error Propagation, Taylor Expansion, and Extrema

    This section develops three closely related applications of partial derivatives and the Hessian matrix. We begin with error propagation: using the gradient to bound how measurement uncertainties in the inputs propagate to an uncertainty in a computed output. We then derive and apply the multivariate Taylor expansion, which approximates a smooth function near a given point by a polynomial involving the gradient and the Hessian. Finally, we use the second-order Taylor polynomial to classify local extrema: the Hessian matrix tells us whether a critical point is a local minimum, local maximum, or saddle point.
    🔗

    Subsection 2.2.1 Error Propagation

    In engineering experiments, every measured quantity carries an uncertainty. When a derived result is computed from several measurements, those individual uncertainties combine to produce an uncertainty in the final result. The gradient provides a systematic tool for quantifying this effect: it linearises the output function around the measured point, turning the problem of estimating the output error into a straightforward evaluation of partial derivatives.
    🔗

    Remark 2.2.1. The Idea of Error Propagation.

    Consider a derived quantity \(U = U(x_1, \ldots, x_n)\) computed from \(n\) measured variables. Write the true (unknown) input as \(\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_0 + \Delta\mathbf{x}\text{,}\) where \(\mathbf{x}_0\) is the measured (nominal) value and \(\Delta\mathbf{x} = (\Delta_1, \ldots, \Delta_n)^\top\) is the measurement uncertainty.
    🔗
    The resulting error in \(U\) is
    \begin{equation*} \Delta U = U(\mathbf{x}_0 + \Delta\mathbf{x}) - U(\mathbf{x}_0). \end{equation*}
    Replacing this difference by its first-order linear approximation (the tangent plane at \(\mathbf{x}_0\text{,}\) cf. Remark 2.1.36):
    \begin{equation*} \Delta U \approx \nabla U(\mathbf{x}_0) \cdot \Delta\mathbf{x} = \frac{\partial U}{\partial x_1}(\mathbf{x}_0)\,\Delta_1 + \cdots + \frac{\partial U}{\partial x_n}(\mathbf{x}_0)\,\Delta_n. \end{equation*}
    Taking absolute values and applying the triangle inequality to this linear approximation yields the first-order estimate
    \begin{equation*} |\Delta U| \approx \left|\frac{\partial U}{\partial x_1}(\mathbf{x}_0)\right| |\Delta_1| + \cdots + \left|\frac{\partial U}{\partial x_n}(\mathbf{x}_0)\right| |\Delta_n|. \end{equation*}
    Each partial derivative acts as a sensitivity factor: it tells us how strongly a small change in \(x_i\) affects the output \(U\text{.}\) The estimate becomes more reliable when the measurement errors are small and higher-order terms can be neglected.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 2.2.2. Maximal Absolute and Relative Error.

    Let \(U = U(x_1, \ldots, x_n)\) be differentiable at \(\mathbf{x}_0 = (x_{1,0}, \ldots, x_{n,0})\text{,}\) and let \(\Delta_1, \ldots, \Delta_n\) be small measurement errors. All partial derivatives below are evaluated at \(\mathbf{x}_0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    • Maximal absolute error: the exact error is \(|\Delta U| = |U(\mathbf{x}_0 + \Delta\mathbf{x}) - U(\mathbf{x}_0)|\text{.}\) Its first-order estimate is
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} \left|\frac{\partial U}{\partial x_1}\right| |\Delta_1| + \cdots + \left|\frac{\partial U}{\partial x_n}\right| |\Delta_n|. \end{equation*}
      🔗
    • Maximal relative error: provided \(U(\mathbf{x}_0) \neq 0\text{,}\) the exact relative error is \(\left|\frac{\Delta U}{U}\right| = \left|\frac{U(\mathbf{x}_0 + \Delta\mathbf{x}) - U(\mathbf{x}_0)}{U(\mathbf{x}_0)}\right|\text{.}\) Its first-order estimate is
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} \left|\frac{1}{U}\frac{\partial U}{\partial x_1}\right| |\Delta_1| + \cdots + \left|\frac{1}{U}\frac{\partial U}{\partial x_n}\right| |\Delta_n|. \end{equation*}
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.2.3. Error Propagation in a Free-Fall Experiment.

    A ball is dropped from rest. The height of fall \(h\) and the travel time \(t\) are measured, and the gravitational acceleration is computed from \(h = \tfrac{1}{2}gt^2\text{,}\) giving
    \begin{equation*} g(h, t) = \frac{2h}{t^2}. \end{equation*}
    The measurements and their uncertainties are
    \begin{equation*} h = 800\,\text{mm} \pm 4\,\text{mm}, \qquad t = 0.4046\,\text{s} \pm 0.0001\,\text{s}. \end{equation*}
    What is the resulting uncertainty in \(g\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Step 1: partial derivatives of \(g\text{.}\)
    \begin{gather*} \frac{\partial g}{\partial h} = \frac{2}{t^2}, \qquad \frac{\partial g}{\partial t} = -\frac{4h}{t^3}. \end{gather*}
    🔗
    Step 2: bound the absolute error. Using the error propagation formula with \(h_0 = 800\,\text{mm}\text{,}\) \(t_0 = 0.4046\,\text{s}\text{,}\) \(|\Delta h| = 4\,\text{mm}\text{,}\) and \(|\Delta t| = 0.0001\,\text{s}\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} |\Delta g| &\leq \left|\frac{2}{(0.4046)^2}\right| \cdot 4 + \left|\frac{-4 \cdot 800}{(0.4046)^3}\right| \cdot 0.0001\\ &\approx 53.7\,\frac{\text{mm}}{\text{s}^2} = 0.0537\,\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Result. The nominal value is \(g_0 = 2 \cdot 0.8 / (0.4046)^2 \approx 9.773\,\text{m}/\text{s}^2\text{,}\) so the estimated gravitational acceleration with its uncertainty is
    \begin{equation*} g = (9.773 \pm 0.054)\,\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}. \end{equation*}
    The dominant contribution comes from the height measurement, since the sensitivity factor \(|\partial g / \partial h|\) multiplied by \(|\Delta h|\) is substantially larger than the time contribution.
    🔗
    This example also shows a practical engineering lesson: improving the precision of the measurement with the largest sensitivity contribution gives the biggest reduction in the final output uncertainty.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.2.2 Multivariate Taylor Expansion

    In single-variable calculus, the Taylor polynomial provides the best polynomial approximation of a smooth function near a given point. The same idea extends to functions of several variables: the second-order Taylor polynomial replaces the tangent line by a tangent paraboloid, capturing not just the slope but also the curvature of the function near the expansion point. This quadratic approximation is the foundation for the extremum classification developed in Subsection 2.2.3.
    🔗

    Remark 2.2.4. Derivation of the Multivariate Taylor Formula.

    Let \(f \colon D \to \R\) with \(D \subseteq \R^n\text{,}\) and fix a base point \(\mathbf{x}_0 \in D\) and a direction \(\mathbf{v} \in \R^n\text{.}\) Assume that \(f\) has continuous second-order partial derivatives near \(\mathbf{x}_0\text{.}\) Consider the single-variable function \(h(t) = f(\mathbf{x}_0 + t\mathbf{v})\text{.}\) Applying the univariate second-order Taylor formula to \(h\) at \(t = 0\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} h(t) = h(0) + h'(0)\,t + \frac{h''(0)}{2}\,t^2 + \mathcal{O}(t^3). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We compute the derivatives of \(h\) at \(t = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    First derivative: By the definition of the directional derivative (cf. Definition 2.1.44):
    \begin{equation*} h'(0) = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{f(\mathbf{x}_0 + t\mathbf{v}) - f(\mathbf{x}_0)}{t} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{x}_0) = \nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0) \cdot \mathbf{v}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Second derivative: Differentiating once more, the chain rule gives
    \begin{equation*} h''(0) = \mathbf{v}^\top H_f(\mathbf{x}_0)\, \mathbf{v} = \sum_{i,j=1}^{n} v_i\, \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i \partial x_j}(\mathbf{x}_0)\, v_j, \end{equation*}
    where \(H_f(\mathbf{x}_0)\) is the Hessian matrix at \(\mathbf{x}_0\) (cf. Definition 2.1.54). The expression \(\mathbf{v}^\top H_f(\mathbf{x}_0)\, \mathbf{v}\) is the quadratic form associated with the Hessian, evaluated at \(\mathbf{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Setting \(t = 1\) and writing \(\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_0 + \mathbf{v}\) (so \(\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_0\)) yields the multivariate Taylor expansion, with a remainder that is small compared with \(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0\|^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 2.2.5. Multivariate Taylor Expansion of Order 2.

    Let \(f \colon D \to \R\) be a function of \(n\) variables whose second-order partial derivatives exist and are continuous on an open set \(D \subseteq \R^n\text{.}\) Let \(\mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{x} \in D\) be such that the line segment connecting them lies entirely in \(D\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} f(\mathbf{x}) = f(\mathbf{x}_0) + \nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0) \cdot (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_0) + \tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0)^\top H_f(\mathbf{x}_0) (\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0) + R_2(\mathbf{x}), \end{equation*}
    where the remainder satisfies
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{\mathbf{x} \to \mathbf{x}_0} \frac{R_2(\mathbf{x})}{\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_0\|^2} = 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Explicitly, the three terms of the Taylor polynomial are:
    • Zeroth order: \(f(\mathbf{x}_0)\) — the function value at the expansion point.
      🔗
      🔗
    • First order: \(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0) \cdot (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_0)\) — the tangent plane correction (linear term).
      🔗
      🔗
    • Second order: \(\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0)^\top H_f(\mathbf{x}_0)(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0)\) — the curvature correction (quadratic term).
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.2.6. Taylor Polynomial of \(e^{x+y^2}\) at the Origin.

    Determine the second-order Taylor polynomial of \(f(x,y) = e^{x + y^2}\) at \(\mathbf{x}_0 = (0,0)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 1: function value.
    \begin{equation*} f(0,0) = e^0 = 1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Step 2: gradient at \((0,0)\text{.}\)
    \begin{gather*} \nabla f(x,y) = \begin{pmatrix} e^{x+y^2} \\ 2y\,e^{x+y^2} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \nabla f(0,0) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}. \end{gather*}
    🔗
    Step 3: Hessian matrix at \((0,0)\text{.}\) The second-order partial derivatives of \(f\) are:
    \begin{align*} \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} &= e^{x+y^2},\\ \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x \partial y} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y \partial x} &= 2y\,e^{x+y^2},\\ \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2} &= (2 + 4y^2)\,e^{x+y^2}. \end{align*}
    Evaluating at \((0,0)\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} H_f(0,0) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Step 4: assemble the Taylor polynomial.
    \begin{align*} f(x,y) &\approx f(0,0) + \nabla f(0,0) \cdot \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} + \tfrac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} x & y \end{pmatrix} H_f(0,0) \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}\\ &= 1 + x + \tfrac{1}{2}(x^2 + 2y^2). \end{align*}
    🔗
    The quadratic term \(\tfrac{1}{2}(x^2 + 2y^2)\) encodes the curvature of the surface: the factor \(2\) on \(y^2\) reflects that \(f\) curves twice as steeply in the \(y\)-direction as in the \(x\)-direction near the origin.
    🔗
    Since \(e^{x+y^2} = 1 + x + y^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}x^2 + \text{higher-order terms}\text{,}\) this result also agrees with the familiar single-variable series for \(e^u\) after substituting \(u = x + y^2\) and discarding all terms of degree at least \(3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 2.2.7. The Taylor Polynomial as a Local Approximation.

    Near \(\mathbf{x}_0\text{,}\) the second-order Taylor polynomial approximates \(f\) by a paraboloid: an elliptic paraboloid when \(H_f(\mathbf{x}_0)\) is definite (positive or negative), or a hyperbolic paraboloid (saddle shape) when \(H_f(\mathbf{x}_0)\) is indefinite. This geometric picture motivates the extremum classification in Subsection 2.2.3: the type of paraboloid that best fits the graph of \(f\) at a critical point determines whether that point is a local minimum, maximum, or saddle point.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.2.3 Local Extrema of Multivariable Functions

    One of the central applications of differential calculus is finding where a function attains its largest or smallest values. For a single-variable function \(f\text{,}\) a necessary condition for a local extremum at \(x_0\) is that the tangent line is horizontal: \(f'(x_0) = 0\text{.}\) The analogous condition in higher dimensions requires that the tangent plane is horizontal, i.e., the gradient vanishes. To distinguish minima, maxima, and saddle points among the critical points, we analyse the second-order Taylor polynomial via the Hessian matrix.
    🔗

    Definition 2.2.8. Local Maximum, Minimum, and Extreme Point.

    Let \(U \subset \R^n\) and \(f \colon U \to \R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    • \(f\) has a local maximum (respectively local minimum) at \(\mathbf{x} \in U\) if there exists an open neighbourhood \(V \subset U\) of \(\mathbf{x}\) such that
      \begin{equation*} f(\mathbf{x}) \geq f(\mathbf{y}) \quad \text{(resp. } f(\mathbf{x}) \leq f(\mathbf{y})\text{)} \quad \forall\, \mathbf{y} \in V. \end{equation*}
      If the inequality is strict (\(\gt\) resp. \(\lt\)), the extremum is called strict (or isolated).
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\mathbf{x}\) is a local extreme point if \(f\) has a local maximum or minimum there.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 2.2.9. Necessary Condition for a Local Extremum.

    Let \(U \subset \R^n\) be open and let \(f \colon U \to \R\) be partially differentiable at \(\mathbf{x}_0 \in U\text{.}\) If \(f\) has a local extreme point at \(\mathbf{x}_0\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} \nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0) = \mathbf{0}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    A point at which \(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0) = \mathbf{0}\) is called a critical point of \(f\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    Fix any coordinate direction \(\mathbf{e}_i\) and consider the single-variable function \(g_i(t) = f(\mathbf{x}_0 + t\mathbf{e}_i)\) for \(t\) close to \(0\text{.}\) Because \(\mathbf{x}_0\) is a local extremum of \(f\text{,}\) the point \(t=0\) is a local extremum of \(g_i\text{.}\) The single-variable necessary condition therefore gives \(g_i'(0)=0\text{.}\) But \(g_i'(0) = \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(\mathbf{x}_0)\text{.}\) Since this holds for every \(i = 1,\ldots,n\text{,}\) all partial derivatives vanish, so \(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0)=\mathbf{0}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 2.2.10. Critical Points Need Not Be Extreme Points.

    As in the single-variable case, the vanishing of the gradient is necessary but not sufficient for an extremum. The standard example is the saddle point: for \(f(x,y) = x^2 - y^2\text{,}\) the origin satisfies \(\nabla f(0,0) = \mathbf{0}\text{,}\) yet \(f\) increases from the origin in the \(x\)-direction and decreases in the \(y\)-direction, so \((0,0)\) is neither a maximum nor a minimum. To determine the nature of a critical point, we must look at second-order information.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 2.2.11. Sufficient Condition via the Taylor Polynomial.

    Let \(\mathbf{x}_0\) be a critical point of \(f\text{,}\) so \(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0) = \mathbf{0}\text{.}\) Applying the second-order Taylor expansion (cf. Theorem 2.2.5), the linear term vanishes and we obtain
    \begin{equation*} f(\mathbf{x}) \approx f(\mathbf{x}_0) + \frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0)^\top H_f(\mathbf{x}_0) (\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0). \end{equation*}
    The sign of \(f(\mathbf{x}) - f(\mathbf{x}_0)\) near \(\mathbf{x}_0\) is therefore determined by the quadratic form \(q(\mathbf{d}) = \mathbf{d}^\top H_f(\mathbf{x}_0)\,\mathbf{d}\) with \(\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_0\text{:}\)
    🔗
    • If \(q(\mathbf{d}) \gt 0\) for every nonzero \(\mathbf{d} \in \R^n\) (i.e., \(H_f(\mathbf{x}_0)\) is positive definite), then \(f(\mathbf{x}) \gt f(\mathbf{x}_0)\) for nearby \(\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{x}_0\text{:}\) \(f\) has a strict local minimum at \(\mathbf{x}_0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(q(\mathbf{d}) \lt 0\) for every nonzero \(\mathbf{d} \in \R^n\) (i.e., \(H_f(\mathbf{x}_0)\) is negative definite), then \(f(\mathbf{x}) \lt f(\mathbf{x}_0)\) for nearby \(\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{x}_0\text{:}\) \(f\) has a strict local maximum at \(\mathbf{x}_0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(q\) takes both positive and negative values (i.e., \(H_f(\mathbf{x}_0)\) is indefinite), then \(f\) increases in some directions and decreases in others: \(f\) has a saddle point at \(\mathbf{x}_0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    If the Hessian is only semi-definite, the quadratic term alone is not decisive; then higher-order terms must be examined.
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 2.2.13. Classification of Critical Points via the Hessian.

    Let \(f \colon D \to \R\) be a function of \(n\) variables whose second-order partial derivatives exist and are continuous. Let \(\mathbf{x}_0\) be a critical point, i.e., \(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0) = \mathbf{0}\text{.}\)
    • \(H_f(\mathbf{x}_0)\) positive definite \(\implies\) \(f\) has a strict local minimum at \(\mathbf{x}_0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(H_f(\mathbf{x}_0)\) negative definite \(\implies\) \(f\) has a strict local maximum at \(\mathbf{x}_0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(H_f(\mathbf{x}_0)\) indefinite \(\implies\) \(f\) has a saddle point at \(\mathbf{x}_0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    If \(H_f(\mathbf{x}_0)\) is positive or negative semi-definite (but not definite), the test is inconclusive.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    By the second-order Taylor formula,
    \begin{equation*} f(\mathbf{x}) = f(\mathbf{x}_0) + \frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0)^\top H_f(\mathbf{x}_0)(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0) + R_2(\mathbf{x}), \end{equation*}
    because the linear term vanishes at a critical point. The remainder satisfies \(R_2(\mathbf{x}) = o(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0\|^2)\text{.}\) Hence, for \(\mathbf{x}\) sufficiently close to \(\mathbf{x}_0\text{,}\) the sign of \(f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x}_0)\) is governed by the quadratic form associated with the Hessian. Positive definiteness gives positivity for every nonzero displacement and therefore a strict local minimum; negative definiteness gives a strict local maximum; and an indefinite Hessian yields nearby directions with both positive and negative change, so the point is a saddle point.
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 2.2.14. Hessian Determinant Criterion for Functions of Two Variables.

    For a function \(f \colon D \to \R\) of two variables with continuous second-order partial derivatives, let \((x_0, y_0)\) be a critical point. Write
    \begin{equation*} H_f(x_0, y_0) = \begin{pmatrix} f_{xx} & f_{xy} \\ f_{yx} & f_{yy} \end{pmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    where \(f_{xx} = \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}(x_0,y_0)\text{,}\) etc.
    🔗
    • If \(\det H_f(x_0,y_0) \gt 0\) and \(f_{xx}(x_0,y_0) \gt 0\text{,}\) then \((x_0,y_0)\) is a strict local minimum.
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(\det H_f(x_0,y_0) \gt 0\) and \(f_{xx}(x_0,y_0) \lt 0\text{,}\) then \((x_0,y_0)\) is a strict local maximum.
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(\det H_f(x_0,y_0) \lt 0\text{,}\) then \((x_0,y_0)\) is a saddle point.
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(\det H_f(x_0,y_0) = 0\text{,}\) the criterion is inconclusive.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 2.2.15. Practical Procedure for Two-Variable Extrema.

    To find and classify all local extrema of a smooth function \(f(x,y)\text{:}\)
    🔗
    1. Find critical points: solve \(\nabla f(x,y) = \mathbf{0}\text{,}\) i.e., both equations \(f_x = 0\) and \(f_y = 0\) simultaneously.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Compute the Hessian at each critical point: calculate \(f_{xx}\text{,}\) \(f_{xy}\text{,}\) and \(f_{yy}\) and evaluate at the critical point.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Apply the determinant criterion (Theorem 2.2.14): evaluate \(\det H_f = f_{xx} f_{yy} - f_{xy}^2\) and check the sign of \(f_{xx}\) if \(\det H_f \gt 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.2.16. Critical Points of \(e^{-x^2/2}\cosh(y)\).

    Consider \(f(x,y) = e^{-x^2/2} \cosh(y)\text{.}\) Find and classify all local extreme points.
    🔗
    Step 1: find critical points.
    \begin{gather*} \nabla f(x,y) = \begin{pmatrix} -x\,e^{-x^2/2} \cosh(y) \\ e^{-x^2/2} \sinh(y) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}. \end{gather*}
    Since \(e^{-x^2/2} \gt 0\) always and \(\cosh(y) \geq 1 \gt 0\) always, the first equation requires \(x = 0\text{.}\) With \(x = 0\text{,}\) the second equation becomes \(\sinh(y) = 0\text{,}\) which holds only for \(y = 0\text{.}\) The unique critical point is \((0,0)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 2: Hessian at \((0,0)\text{.}\)
    \begin{align*} f_{xx}(x,y) &= (-1 + x^2)\,e^{-x^2/2}\cosh(y),\\ f_{xy}(x,y) &= -x\,e^{-x^2/2}\sinh(y),\\ f_{yy}(x,y) &= e^{-x^2/2}\cosh(y). \end{align*}
    Evaluating at \((0,0)\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} H_f(0,0) = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Step 3: classification. \(\det H_f(0,0) = (-1)(1) - 0^2 = -1 \lt 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    By Theorem 2.2.14, the function \(f\) has a saddle point at \((0,0)\text{.}\) The surface rises in the \(y\)-direction (where \(f_{yy} = 1 \gt 0\)) and falls in the \(x\)-direction (where \(f_{xx} = -1 \lt 0\)).
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.2.17. Critical Points of \(x(\ln y - 2x)\).

    Consider \(f(x,y) = x\,(\ln y - 2x)\) on the domain \(\{(x,y) : y \gt 0\}\text{.}\) Find and classify all local extreme points.
    🔗
    Step 1: find critical points.
    \begin{gather*} \nabla f(x,y) = \begin{pmatrix} \ln y - 4x \\[3pt] x/y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}. \end{gather*}
    The second equation gives \(x = 0\) (since \(y \gt 0\)). Substituting into the first equation: \(\ln y = 0 \implies y = 1\text{.}\) The unique critical point is \((0,1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 2: Hessian at \((0,1)\text{.}\)
    \begin{gather*} f_{xx} = -4, \quad f_{xy} = \frac{1}{y}, \quad f_{yy} = -\frac{x}{y^2}. \end{gather*}
    Evaluating at \((0,1)\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} H_f(0,1) = \begin{pmatrix} -4 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Step 3: classification. \(\det H_f(0,1) = (-4)(0) - 1^2 = -1 \lt 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    By Theorem 2.2.14, \(f\) has a saddle point at \((0,1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.2.18. Local Minimum of a Paraboloid.

    Consider \(f(x,y) = x^2 + xy + y^2 + 2x - y\text{.}\) Find and classify all local extreme points.
    🔗
    Step 1: find critical points.
    \begin{gather*} f_x = 2x + y + 2 = 0, \qquad f_y = x + 2y - 1 = 0. \end{gather*}
    From the first equation, \(y = -2x - 2\text{.}\) Substituting into the second: \(x + 2(-2x-2) - 1 = 0 \implies -3x - 5 = 0 \implies x = -\tfrac{5}{3}\text{.}\) Then \(y = -2(-\tfrac{5}{3}) - 2 = \tfrac{4}{3}\text{.}\) The unique critical point is \(\bigl(-\tfrac{5}{3},\tfrac{4}{3}\bigr)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 2: Hessian. Since \(f_{xx} = 2\text{,}\) \(f_{xy} = 1\text{,}\) \(f_{yy} = 2\) are constant:
    \begin{equation*} H_f = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Step 3: classification. \(\det H_f = 4 - 1 = 3 \gt 0\) and \(f_{xx} = 2 \gt 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    By Theorem 2.2.14, \(f\) has a strict local minimum at \(\bigl(-\tfrac{5}{3},\tfrac{4}{3}\bigr)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The workflow for local extrema is now complete: find the critical points from \(\nabla f = \mathbf{0}\text{,}\) then use the Hessian to decide whether the second-order Taylor polynomial behaves like a bowl, an upside-down bowl, or a saddle. This gives a direct bridge from local approximation to optimisation.

    1. Interpret an Error Estimate.

    In the first-order estimate
    \begin{equation*} |\Delta U| \approx \left|\frac{\partial U}{\partial x_1}\right||\Delta_1| + \cdots + \left|\frac{\partial U}{\partial x_n}\right||\Delta_n|, \end{equation*}
    which input contributes the most to the output uncertainty?
    🔗
    • The one with the largest product \(\left|\frac{\partial U}{\partial x_i}\right||\Delta_i|\text{.}\)
    • Correct. The sensitivity factor and the size of the input uncertainty matter together.
    • Always the variable with the largest measured value.
    • Incorrect. A large nominal value alone does not determine the propagated error.
    • Always the variable with the smallest uncertainty.
    • Incorrect. A smaller uncertainty usually contributes less, not more.
    • All variables contribute equally.
    • Incorrect. The contribution depends on both sensitivity and uncertainty size.

    2. Estimate an Absolute Error.

    Let
    \begin{equation*} U(x,y)=xy. \end{equation*}
    At the measured point \((x_0,y_0)=(2,3)\text{,}\) the uncertainties are \(|\Delta x|=0.1\) and \(|\Delta y|=0.2\text{.}\) Use the first-order formula to estimate the maximal absolute error in \(U\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We compute the partial derivatives:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial U}{\partial x}=y, \qquad \frac{\partial U}{\partial y}=x. \end{equation*}
    At \((2,3)\text{,}\) this gives
    \begin{equation*} \left.\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}\right|_{(2,3)}=3, \qquad \left.\frac{\partial U}{\partial y}\right|_{(2,3)}=2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Therefore the first-order absolute error estimate is
    \begin{equation*} |\Delta U| \approx |3|\cdot 0.1 + |2|\cdot 0.2 = 0.3 + 0.4 = 0.7. \end{equation*}
    So the estimated maximal absolute error is \(0.7\text{.}\)

    3. Second-Order Taylor Polynomial.

    Determine the second-order Taylor polynomial at the origin for
    \begin{equation*} f(x,y)=e^x+y^2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    We have
    \begin{equation*} f(0,0)=1. \end{equation*}
    The first-order partial derivatives are
    \begin{equation*} f_x=e^x, \qquad f_y=2y, \end{equation*}
    so
    \begin{equation*} \nabla f(0,0)=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The second-order partial derivatives are
    \begin{equation*} f_{xx}=e^x, \qquad f_{xy}=f_{yx}=0, \qquad f_{yy}=2. \end{equation*}
    Hence
    \begin{equation*} H_f(0,0)= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Therefore
    \begin{align*} T_2(x,y) &= 1 + \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix} + \tfrac12 \begin{pmatrix}x & y\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}\\ &= 1 + x + \tfrac12 x^2 + y^2. \end{align*}
    🔗

    4. Read the Hessian Criterion.

    Let \((x_0,y_0)\) be a critical point of a twice continuously differentiable function of two variables. If
    \begin{equation*} \det H_f(x_0,y_0) < 0, \end{equation*}
    what does the Hessian criterion say?
    🔗
    • \((x_0,y_0)\) is a strict local minimum.
    • Incorrect. A negative determinant means the Hessian is indefinite.
    • \((x_0,y_0)\) is a strict local maximum.
    • Incorrect. A strict maximum would require a negative definite Hessian, not just a negative determinant.
    • \((x_0,y_0)\) is a saddle point.
    • Correct. In two variables, \(\det H_f(x_0,y_0) < 0\) means the quadratic form takes both signs.
    • The test is inconclusive.
    • Incorrect. The test is inconclusive when the determinant is \(0\text{,}\) not when it is negative.
