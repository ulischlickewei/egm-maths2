    Section 2.1 Functions, Continuity, and Partial Derivatives

    This section extends the core ideas of single-variable calculus — domains, continuity, and differentiation — to functions that depend on several real variables simultaneously. We begin by defining what such functions are and how to describe their graphs and level sets geometrically. We then introduce the sequence-based definition of continuity in \(\R^n\) and develop practical tools for proving that built-up functions are continuous. Finally, we study how to differentiate functions of several variables: partial derivatives with respect to individual coordinates, the gradient as the vector of all partial derivatives, total differentiability and the tangent plane, directional derivatives, and the second-order Hessian matrix.
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.1 Functions of Several Variables

    In mathematics and engineering, many quantities depend simultaneously on several inputs. The temperature at a point in a room is a function of the three position coordinates; the pressure of an ideal gas depends on both its volume and its temperature. This subsection introduces real functions of several variables, discusses how their domains are determined, and explores two ways to visualise them: the graph as a surface in space, and the contour plot via level curves.
    🔗

    Definition 2.1.1. Real Function of Several Variables.

    Let \(D_f \subset \R^n\text{.}\) A map
    \begin{equation*} f \colon D_f \to \R \end{equation*}
    is called a real function of several variables. The set \(D_f\) is the domain of definition of \(f\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.1.2. Two Basic Examples.

    • \(f \colon \R^2 \to \R\text{,}\) \(f(x,y) = x \sin y\text{.}\) For instance, \(f(1,1) = \sin 1\) and \(f\!\left(1, \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right) = 1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(g \colon D_g \to \R\text{,}\) \((x_1, x_2, x_3) \mapsto \sqrt{36 - x_1^2 - x_2^2 - x_3^2}\text{.}\) The square root requires a non-negative argument, so
      \begin{equation*} D_g = \left\{ (x_1, x_2, x_3) \in \R^3 \;\middle|\; x_1^2 + x_2^2 + x_3^2 \le 36 \right\}, \end{equation*}
      the closed ball of radius \(6\) centred at the origin.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.1.3. Functions of Several Variables in Engineering and Physics.

    Multivariable functions appear throughout applied disciplines:
    🔗
    • Harmonic oscillation: \(x(A, \omega, t, \varphi) = A \cos(\omega t + \varphi)\text{,}\) where \(A\) is the amplitude, \(\omega\) the angular frequency, and \(\varphi\) the phase shift.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Mechanical power: \(P(W,t) = W/t\text{,}\) where \(W\) is work and \(t\) is time.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Ideal gas law: \(p(V,T) = RT/V\text{,}\) expressing pressure as a function of volume and temperature.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 2.1.4. Visualisation of Functions of Two Variables.

    There are two standard ways to visualise a function \(f \colon \R^2 \to \R\text{:}\)
    🔗
    • The graph is the surface
      \begin{equation*} G_f = \left\{ (x, y, z) \in \R^3 \mid z = f(x,y) \right\}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    • A contour plot displays several level curves \(\{(x,y) \mid f(x,y) = c\}\) for chosen constants \(c\text{.}\) This is analogous to elevation contour lines on a topographic map.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    We illustrate both visualisation methods with two concrete examples. For the paraboloid \(f(x,y)=x^2+y^2\text{,}\) the graph is a bowl-shaped surface and the level curves are circles. For the saddle function \(g(x,y)=x^2-y^2\text{,}\) the graph bends upward in one direction and downward in the other, while the level curves are hyperbolas.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.1.9. The Upper Unit Semi-sphere as a Graph.

    We investigate the function \(f \colon D_f \to \R\) whose graph is the upper hemisphere of the unit sphere.
    🔗
    The domain must consist of all base points \((x,y)\) that lie under the hemisphere:
    \begin{equation*} D_f = \{ (x,y) \in \R^2 \mid x^2 + y^2 \le 1 \}. \end{equation*}
    For a point \((x,y) \in D_f\text{,}\) the height \(z \ge 0\) satisfies \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1\text{,}\) giving
    \begin{equation*} f(x,y) = \sqrt{1 - x^2 - y^2}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 2.1.11. Surface of Revolution.

    Rotating the graph \(z = g(r)\) of a function \(g \colon \R_{\ge 0} \to \R\) around the \(z\)-axis produces a surface of revolution. In terms of Cartesian coordinates, this surface is the graph of
    \begin{equation*} f(x,y) = g\!\left(\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\right). \end{equation*}
    The function value depends only on the radial distance to the \(z\)-axis, which explains the rotational symmetry.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.1.12. A First Surface of Revolution.

    Consider the function \(g \colon \R_{\ge 0} \to \R\text{,}\) \(g(r) = \dfrac{1}{1+r^2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Rotating the graph of \(g\) around the \(z\)-axis yields the surface
    \begin{equation*} f(x,y) = \frac{1}{1+x^2+y^2}. \end{equation*}
    This example shows directly how a one-variable function of the radius becomes a function of two variables.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.1.14. A Surface of Revolution as the Graph of a Two-Variable Function.

    We investigate the function by first showing that its one-variable profile can be extended continuously at the origin and then by rotating this profile around the \(z\)-axis to obtain a surface of revolution.
    🔗
    Consider \(g \colon \R \setminus \{0\} \to \R\text{,}\) \(g(x) = \dfrac{\sin(x^2)}{x^2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. Extending \(g\) continuously to all of \(\R\text{.}\) Using the Taylor series \(\sin(u) = u - u^3/6 + \cdots\) with \(u = x^2\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} g(x) = \frac{\sin(x^2)}{x^2} = \frac{x^2 - x^6/6 + \cdots}{x^2} = 1 - \frac{x^4}{6} + \cdots \;\xrightarrow{x \to 0}\; 1. \end{equation*}
      Setting \(g(0) = 1\) extends \(g\) continuously to all of \(\R\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Describing the surface of revolution. Rotating the graph of \(g\) around the \(z\)-axis replaces the distance \(|x|\) from the axis by the radial distance \(r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\text{.}\) The resulting surface is the graph of
      \begin{equation*} f(x,y) = \frac{\sin(x^2 + y^2)}{x^2 + y^2}, \qquad (x,y) \ne (0,0), \end{equation*}
      extended by \(f(0,0) = 1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.2 Continuity

    Continuity for a function of several variables means that nearby inputs produce nearby outputs — exactly as in one dimension. The key new feature is that a point \(\mathbf{x}_0 \in \R^n\) can be approached from infinitely many directions, and the function must return the same limit along every approach path. We make this precise using sequences, because the same sequence definition used for single-variable limits carries over without change.
    🔗

    Definition 2.1.16. Limit of a Sequence in \(\R^n\).

    Let \((\mathbf{x}_m)_{m \in \N}\) be a sequence in \(\R^n\) and let \(\mathbf{x} \in \R^n\text{.}\) We say that \((\mathbf{x}_m)\) converges to \(\mathbf{x}\text{,}\) written
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{m \to \infty} \mathbf{x}_m = \mathbf{x}, \end{equation*}
    if for every \(\varepsilon > 0\) there exists \(m_0\) such that for all \(m \ge m_0\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \| \mathbf{x}_m - \mathbf{x} \| \lt \varepsilon. \end{equation*}
    Geometrically, \(\mathbf{x}_m\) eventually lies inside every open ball of radius \(\varepsilon\) centred at \(\mathbf{x}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 2.1.18. Componentwise Convergence.

    If \((\mathbf{x}_m) = (x_{m,1}, \ldots, x_{m,n})\) is a sequence in \(\R^n\text{,}\) then it converges to \(\mathbf{x}\) if and only if each coordinate sequence converges: \(x_{m,i} \to x_i\) for \(i = 1, \ldots, n\text{.}\) This reduces questions about sequences in \(\R^n\) to \(n\) familiar one-dimensional problems.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.1.19. A Sequence in \(\R^3\).

    The sequence
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}_n = \left(\frac{1}{n},\; 2 - \frac{3n + 4n^2}{6n^3 + \ln n},\; e^{-n}\right) \end{equation*}
    in \(\R^3\) satisfies \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \mathbf{x}_n = (0, 2, 0)\text{,}\) since each coordinate converges: \(1/n \to 0\text{,}\) \((3n + 4n^2)/(6n^3 + \ln n) \to 0\text{,}\) and \(e^{-n} \to 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 2.1.20. Limit of a Multivariate Function.

    Let \(f \colon D_f \to \R\text{,}\) and let \(\mathbf{x}_0\) be an accumulation point of \(D_f\text{.}\) We say that \(f\) has limit \(z_0\) at \(\mathbf{x}_0\) if for every sequence \((\mathbf{x}_m) \subset D_f \setminus \{\mathbf{x}_0\}\) with \(\lim_{m \to \infty} \mathbf{x}_m = \mathbf{x}_0\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{m \to \infty} f(\mathbf{x}_m) = z_0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For example, for \(f(x,y)=x^2+y^2\) and \(\mathbf{x}_0=(0,0)\text{,}\) every sequence approaching the origin has image values approaching \(0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 2.1.22. Continuity of a Function of \(n\) Variables.

    • A function \(f \colon D_f \to \R\) is continuous at \(\mathbf{x}_0 \in D_f\) if for every sequence \((\mathbf{x}_m)\) in \(D_f\) with \(\lim_{m \to \infty} \mathbf{x}_m = \mathbf{x}_0\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} \lim_{m \to \infty} f(\mathbf{x}_m) = f(\mathbf{x}_0). \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    • A function is continuous if it is continuous at every point of its domain.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    A key feature of multivariate functions is that continuity requires the same limit along every approach path, not only along coordinate axes. The next example shows how to prove discontinuity by finding two sequences approaching the same point with different limits.
    🔗

    Remark 2.1.23. How Path Tests Can and Cannot Be Used.

    If two approach paths give different limiting values, the limit does not exist. However, checking only a few paths can never prove that a limit exists, because there are infinitely many possible ways to approach the point. To prove existence, one needs a general argument, for example by using continuity laws, an estimate, or a theorem.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.1.24. Discontinuity via Two Approach Paths.

    Analyse the continuity of
    \begin{equation*} f(x,y) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{-4x^3 y}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} & (x,y) \ne (0,0), \\[4pt] 0 & (x,y) = (0,0). \end{cases} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For \((x,y) \ne (0,0)\text{,}\) the function is a ratio of polynomials with a non-vanishing denominator, hence continuous. At the origin we test two approach paths:
    🔗
    • Along the \(x\)-axis: \(\mathbf{p}_n = (1/n, 0) \to (0,0)\text{.}\)
      \begin{equation*} f(\mathbf{p}_n) = \frac{-4(1/n)^3 \cdot 0}{((1/n)^2 + 0)^2} = 0 \;\longrightarrow\; 0. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    • Along the diagonal: \(\mathbf{q}_n = (1/n, 1/n) \to (0,0)\text{.}\)
      \begin{equation*} f(\mathbf{q}_n) = \frac{-4(1/n)^3 \cdot (1/n)}{\left((1/n)^2 + (1/n)^2\right)^2} = \frac{-4/n^4}{4/n^4} = -1 \;\longrightarrow\; -1. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Since the limits along the two paths differ, \(\lim_{\mathbf{x} \to (0,0)} f(\mathbf{x})\) does not exist, and \(f\) is not continuous at the origin.
    🔗
    🔗
    The following theorem shows that we can build many continuous functions of several variables from continuous functions of one variable by using sums, products, quotients, and compositions.
    🔗

    Theorem 2.1.26. Laws of Continuity.

    • Constant functions \(f(\mathbf{x}) = c\) are continuous on \(\R^n\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Coordinate functions \(f_i(\mathbf{x}) = x_i\) are continuous on \(\R^n\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(f, g \colon D \subset \R^n \to \R\) are continuous on \(D\text{,}\) then so are \(f + g\text{,}\) \(f - g\text{,}\) and \(f \cdot g\text{;}\) and \(f/g\) is continuous on \(\{\mathbf{x} \in D \mid g(\mathbf{x}) \ne 0\}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Compositions: if \(f \colon D_f \subset \R^n \to \R\) and \(g \colon D_g \subset \R \to \R\) are continuous with \(f(D_f) \subset D_g\text{,}\) then \(g \circ f\) is continuous on \(D_f\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    We use the sequence criterion. Constant and coordinate functions preserve limits of convergent sequences immediately. If \(\mathbf{x}_m \to \mathbf{x}_0\) in \(D\) and \(f(\mathbf{x}_m) \to f(\mathbf{x}_0)\text{,}\) \(g(\mathbf{x}_m) \to g(\mathbf{x}_0)\text{,}\) then the usual one-variable limit laws imply \((f \pm g)(\mathbf{x}_m) \to (f \pm g)(\mathbf{x}_0)\text{,}\) \((fg)(\mathbf{x}_m) \to (fg)(\mathbf{x}_0)\text{,}\) and, provided \(g(\mathbf{x}_0) \ne 0\text{,}\) also \((f/g)(\mathbf{x}_m) \to (f/g)(\mathbf{x}_0)\text{.}\) For compositions, \(\mathbf{x}_m \to \mathbf{x}_0\) implies \(f(\mathbf{x}_m) \to f(\mathbf{x}_0)\text{,}\) and continuity of \(g\) then gives \(g(f(\mathbf{x}_m)) \to g(f(\mathbf{x}_0))\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.1.27. Proving Continuity via the Combination Laws.

    Show that \(f \colon \R^2 \to \R\text{,}\) \(f(x_1, x_2) = x_1 \cdot \sin(x_2)\text{,}\) is continuous.
    🔗
    Define auxiliary functions \(f_1(x_1, x_2) = x_1\) (coordinate function), \(f_2(x_1, x_2) = x_2\) (coordinate function), and \(f_3(x) = \sin(x)\) (a continuous real function). Then \(f = f_1 \cdot (f_3 \circ f_2)\text{.}\) By Theorem 2.1.26, the composition \(f_3 \circ f_2\) is continuous, and the product of two continuous functions is continuous, so \(f\) is continuous on \(\R^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.3 Partial Derivatives, Gradient, and Differentiability

    For a function of a single variable, the derivative measures the rate of change in the only available direction. For a function of several variables we can still measure rates of change, but now we must specify a direction. The simplest choice is to vary one variable while holding all others fixed: this gives the partial derivative. Collecting all partial derivatives into a single vector yields the gradient, which encodes both the magnitude and direction of steepest increase. When the function can be approximated near a point by a linear function involving the gradient, we call it (totally) differentiable at that point.
    🔗

    Remark 2.1.28. Idea of Partial Differentiation.

    For a single-variable function \(z = f(x)\text{,}\) the derivative at \(x_0\) is the limit of slopes of secant lines:
    \begin{equation*} f'(x_0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0)}{h}. \end{equation*}
    This generalises to a function \(f(x,y)\) of two variables as follows: fix \(y = y_0\text{,}\) consider the curve of intersection \(z = f(x, y_0)\) in the \(xz\)-plane, and compute its slope:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x_0, y_0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x_0 + h, y_0) - f(x_0, y_0)}{h}. \end{equation*}
    This is the partial derivative of \(f\) with respect to \(x\) at \((x_0, y_0)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.1.30. Computing a Partial Derivative from the Definition.

    Let \(f(x,y) = x^2 y + 2y\text{.}\) Fix \((x_0, y_0)\) and compute \(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}\big|_{(x_0,y_0)}\) directly from the limit definition.
    🔗
    The slope of the secant through \(f(x_0, y_0)\) and \(f(x_0 + h, y_0)\) is
    \begin{align*} \frac{f(x_0+h, y_0) - f(x_0, y_0)}{h} &= \frac{(x_0+h)^2 y_0 + 2y_0 - x_0^2 y_0 - 2y_0}{h}\\ &= \frac{(x_0^2 + 2hx_0 + h^2)y_0 - x_0^2 y_0}{h} = 2x_0 y_0 + h y_0. \end{align*}
    As \(h \to 0\text{,}\) this converges to \(2x_0 y_0\text{.}\) Thus
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\bigg|_{(x_0, y_0)} = 2x_0 y_0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 2.1.31. Partial Derivative; Partially Differentiable Function.

    Let \(f \colon U \to \R\) be defined on an open set \(U \subset \R^n\text{,}\) let \(\mathbf{x}_0 \in U\text{,}\) and let \(\mathbf{e}_i = (0, \ldots, 0, 1, 0, \ldots, 0)^\top\) be the \(i\)-th standard unit vector of \(\R^n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    • The partial derivative of \(f\) with respect to \(x_i\) at \(\mathbf{x}_0\) is
      \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(\mathbf{x}_0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(\mathbf{x}_0 + h \mathbf{e}_i) - f(\mathbf{x}_0)}{h}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    • To compute \(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\text{,}\) treat all variables except \(x_i\) as constants and differentiate the resulting single-variable function with respect to \(x_i\text{.}\) All standard differentiation rules (product rule, chain rule, etc.) apply.
      🔗
      🔗
    • We call \(f\) partially differentiable on \(D \subseteq \R^n\) if all partial derivatives exist at every point of \(D\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.1.32. Partial Derivatives of a Mixed Exponential.

    Compute the partial derivatives of \(g(x,y) = x e^{x + y^2} + 2xy^4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} &= e^{x+y^2} + x e^{x+y^2} + 2y^4 = (x+1)e^{x+y^2} + 2y^4,\\ \frac{\partial g}{\partial y} &= 2xy e^{x+y^2} + 8xy^3. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 2.1.33. Gradient.

    For a partially differentiable function \(f \colon U \to \R\text{,}\) where \(U \subset \R^n\text{,}\) the gradient of \(f\) at \(\mathbf{x}\) is the column vector of all partial derivatives:
    \begin{equation*} \nabla f(\mathbf{x}) = \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}(\mathbf{x}) \\[4pt] \vdots \\[2pt] \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}(\mathbf{x}) \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.1.34. Gradient of the Normal Paraboloid.

    Let \(f(x,y) = x^2 + y^2\text{.}\) We compute the gradient and then interpret geometrically how it is related to the graph and the level curves of \(f\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \nabla f(x,y) = \begin{pmatrix} \partial f/\partial x \\ \partial f/\partial y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2x \\ 2y \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    At every point \((x,y)\text{,}\) the gradient points radially outwards from the origin — in the direction of steepest ascent on the paraboloid.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 2.1.36. Local Linear Approximation and the Tangent Plane.

    For a single-variable function \(f \colon \R \to \R\text{,}\) the tangent line at \(x_0\) gives the best linear approximation:
    \begin{equation*} t_{x_0}(x) = f(x_0) + f'(x_0)(x - x_0). \end{equation*}
    For a two-variable function \(z = f(x,y)\text{,}\) the tangent line is replaced by the tangent plane. Provided the tangent plane exists at \(P_0 = (x_0, y_0, f(x_0,y_0))\text{,}\) its equation is
    \begin{equation*} t(x,y) = f(x_0,y_0) + \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x_0,y_0)(x - x_0) + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(x_0,y_0)(y - y_0) = f(x_0,y_0) + \nabla f(x_0,y_0)^{\top} \begin{pmatrix} x - x_0 \\ y - y_0 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 2.1.38. Partial Derivatives Can Exist Without a Tangent Plane.

    Existence of all partial derivatives at a point does not guarantee a tangent plane. Consider \(f(x,y) = 1 - \min(|x|, |y|)\text{.}\) In the \(xz\)-plane (\(y = 0\)): \(f(x, 0) = 1 - \min(|x|, 0) = 1\text{,}\) so \(\partial f / \partial x(0,0) = 0\text{.}\) Likewise \(\partial f / \partial y(0,0) = 0\text{.}\) Along the diagonal \((t,t)\) we have \(f(t,t) = 1 - |t|\text{,}\) while the candidate tangent plane from the partial derivatives is the constant plane \(z = 1\text{.}\) Hence the remainder is \(r(t,t) = -|t|\text{,}\) and
    \begin{equation*} \frac{r(t,t)}{\|(t,t)\|} = \frac{-|t|}{\sqrt{2}\,|t|} = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \end{equation*}
    for \(t \ne 0\text{,}\) which does not tend to \(0\text{.}\) So \(f\) is not differentiable at \((0,0)\text{.}\) Yet the graph near the origin cannot be approximated by a plane, because the function behaves differently in oblique directions. Total differentiability is a strictly stronger requirement.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 2.1.40. Differentiable Function.

    • \(f\) is called differentiable (or totally differentiable) at \(\mathbf{x}_0\) if there exists a vector \(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0)\) such that
      \begin{equation*} f(\mathbf{x}) = f(\mathbf{x}_0) + \nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0)^{\top}(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_0) + r(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_0), \end{equation*}
      where the remainder satisfies
      \begin{equation*} \lim_{\mathbf{x} \to \mathbf{x}_0} \frac{r(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_0)}{\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_0\|} = 0. \end{equation*}
      This condition formalises mathematically the geometric idea that \(f\) can be approximated near \(\mathbf{x}_0\) by its tangent plane, because the remainder is negligible compared with the distance to \(\mathbf{x}_0\text{.}\) The linear part is the tangent plane \(t(\mathbf{x}) = f(\mathbf{x}_0) + \nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0)^{\top}(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_0)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 2.1.41. Continuously Partially Differentiable Implies Differentiable.

    If \(f\) is partially differentiable on an open set \(U \subset \R^n\) and all partial derivatives are continuous on \(U\text{,}\) then \(f\) is differentiable on \(U\text{.}\) In other words, every \(C^1\) function is totally differentiable.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.1.42. Tangent Plane and Gradient of a Rational Function.

    Let \(f(x,y) = \dfrac{1}{1 + x^2 + y^2}\text{.}\) In the following, we investigate this function step by step: first its domain and level curves, then its gradient. Finally, we will investigate at which points the plane is parallel to the \(xy\)-plane.
    🔗
    1. Domain. The denominator \(1 + x^2 + y^2 \ge 1 > 0\) for all \((x,y) \in \R^2\text{,}\) so \(D_f = \R^2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Level curves. The level set \(f(x,y) = c\) requires \(1 + x^2 + y^2 = 1/c\text{,}\) i.e.\ \(x^2 + y^2 = 1/c - 1\text{.}\) For \(0 \lt c \lt 1\) these are concentric circles around the origin.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Gradient.
      \begin{align*} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} &= -(1 + x^2 + y^2)^{-2} \cdot 2x = -\frac{2x}{(1 + x^2 + y^2)^2},\\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} &= -\frac{2y}{(1+x^2+y^2)^2}. \end{align*}
      Thus
      \begin{equation*} \nabla f(x,y) = -\frac{2}{(1+x^2+y^2)^2} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
      The gradient points radially towards the origin.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Tangent plane parallel to the \(xy\)-plane. The tangent plane is parallel to the \(xy\)-plane if and only if \(\nabla f(x_0, y_0) = \mathbf{0}\text{.}\) From the formula above this forces \((x_0, y_0) = (0,0)\text{,}\) the unique critical point. At this point \(f(0,0) = 1\text{,}\) so the tangent plane is \(z = 1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.1.43. Linear Approximation of a Three-Variable Function.

    We now determine the linear approximation of the function \(f(x_1, x_2, x_3) = x_1^2 \sin(x_2 - x_3)\) at the point \(\mathbf{x}_0 = (1, 0, 1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    First, \(f(1,0,1) = \sin(-1)\text{.}\) Then:
    \begin{equation*} \nabla f(x_1,x_2,x_3) = \begin{pmatrix} 2x_1 \sin(x_2 - x_3) \\ x_1^2 \cos(x_2 - x_3) \\ -x_1^2 \cos(x_2 - x_3) \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \nabla f(1,0,1) = \begin{pmatrix} 2\sin(-1) \\ \cos(-1) \\ -\cos(-1) \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    The tangent plane (linear approximation) is therefore
    \begin{align*} t(x_1,x_2,x_3) &= \sin(-1) + 2\sin(-1)(x_1-1) + \cos(-1)\, x_2 - \cos(-1)(x_3-1). \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 2.1.44. Directional Derivative.

    Let \(f\) be differentiable at \(\mathbf{x}_0\) and let \(\mathbf{r} = (r_1, \ldots, r_n)^\top\) be a unit vector (\(\|\mathbf{r}\| = 1\)).
    🔗
    • The directional derivative of \(f\) at \(\mathbf{x}_0\) in direction \(\mathbf{r}\) is
      \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{r}}(\mathbf{x}_0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(\mathbf{x}_0 + h\mathbf{r}) - f(\mathbf{x}_0)}{h}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    • For a differentiable function, the directional derivative equals the dot product of the gradient with \(\mathbf{r}\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{r}}(\mathbf{x}_0) = \nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0) \cdot \mathbf{r} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}(\mathbf{x}_0) r_1 + \cdots + \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}(\mathbf{x}_0) r_n. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.1.46. Computing a Directional Derivative.

    Let \(f(x,y) = x^2 e^{x+2y}\) and \((x_0,y_0) = (1,0)\text{.}\) Find the rate of change of \(f\) in the direction \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. Normalise: \(\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{5}\text{,}\) so \(\mathbf{r} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{5}} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Compute the gradient at \((1,0)\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} \nabla f(x,y) = \begin{pmatrix} 2x e^{x+2y} + x^2 e^{x+2y} \\ 2x^2 e^{x+2y} \end{pmatrix}, \quad \nabla f(1,0) = \begin{pmatrix} 3e \\ 2e \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Directional derivative:
      \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{r}}(1,0) = \nabla f(1,0) \cdot \mathbf{r} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}(3e + 4e) = \frac{7e}{\sqrt{5}}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 2.1.47. Geometric Meaning of the Gradient.

    Writing the directional derivative as
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{r}}(\mathbf{x}_0) = \nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0) \cdot \mathbf{r} = \|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0)\| \cos\alpha, \end{equation*}
    where \(\alpha\) is the angle between \(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0)\) and \(\mathbf{r}\text{,}\) reveals two important facts:
    🔗
    • The rate of change is maximised when \(\alpha = 0\text{,}\) i.e., when \(\mathbf{r}\) is parallel to \(\nabla f\text{.}\) Therefore, the gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The rate of change is zero when \(\alpha = \pi/2\text{.}\) Therefore, level curves are everywhere orthogonal to the gradient.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 2.1.49. Application: Gradient Descent in Machine Learning.

    Consider fitting a linear model \(g(x) = mx + b\) to data points \((x_1, y_1), \ldots, (x_N, y_N)\text{.}\) The mean squared error
    \begin{equation*} e(m, b) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \bigl(y_i - (m x_i + b)\bigr)^2 \end{equation*}
    is a function of two variables \(m\) and \(b\text{.}\) The gradient descent algorithm iteratively updates \((m,b)\) in the direction of \(-\nabla e(m,b)\) (steepest descent), reducing the error at each step. This is one of the foundational algorithms in modern machine learning.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.4 Higher-Order Partial Derivatives and the Hessian Matrix

    Just as the derivative of a single-variable function can itself be differentiated to produce the second derivative, the partial derivatives of a multivariate function can be partially differentiated again to produce second-order partial derivatives. There are \(n^2\) such derivatives for a function of \(n\) variables. A fundamental symmetry result — Schwarz’ theorem — states that the order of differentiation does not matter when the mixed derivatives are continuous. Arranging all second-order partial derivatives into a matrix yields the Hessian matrix, which plays the role of the second derivative in the multivariate setting.
    🔗

    Definition 2.1.51. Partial Derivatives of Higher Order.

    Let \(f \colon U \to \R\) be partially differentiable on an open set \(U \subset \R^n\text{.}\) If the partial derivatives \(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(\mathbf{x})\) are themselves partially differentiable, we can form the second-order partial derivatives:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_k \, \partial x_i}(\mathbf{x}), \qquad i, k = 1, \ldots, n. \end{equation*}
    Iterating this process gives partial derivatives of any order.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.1.52. Second-Order Partial Derivatives.

    Compute all second-order partial derivatives of \(f(x,y) = x^3 e^{2y}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    First-order partial derivatives:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 3x^2 e^{2y}, \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 2x^3 e^{2y}. \end{equation*}
    Second-order partial derivatives:
    \begin{align*} \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(3x^2 e^{2y}) = 6x e^{2y},\\ \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y \, \partial x} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(3x^2 e^{2y}) = 6x^2 e^{2y},\\ \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x \, \partial y} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(2x^3 e^{2y}) = 6x^2 e^{2y},\\ \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(2x^3 e^{2y}) = 4x^3 e^{2y}. \end{align*}
    Note that the two mixed partial derivatives are equal: \(\partial^2 f / \partial y\,\partial x = \partial^2 f / \partial x\,\partial y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 2.1.53. Schwarz’ Theorem (Symmetry of Mixed Partials).

    Let \(f \colon U \to \R\) be defined on an open set \(U \subset \R^n\text{.}\) Assume that all second-order partial derivatives of \(f\) exist and are continuous on \(U\text{,}\) and let \(\mathbf{x} \in U\text{.}\) Then for all \(i, k = 1, \ldots, n\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_k \, \partial x_i}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i \, \partial x_k}(\mathbf{x}). \end{equation*}
    The analogous symmetry holds for partial derivatives of third and higher order.
    🔗
    🔗
    As a consequence, the \(n \times n\) matrix of all second-order partial derivatives is symmetric whenever the hypotheses of Schwarz’ theorem are satisfied. This matrix is called the Hessian matrix.
    🔗

    Definition 2.1.54. Hessian Matrix.

    Let \(f \colon U \to \R\) be defined on an open set \(U \subset \R^n\text{,}\) and assume that all second-order partial derivatives of \(f\) are continuous on \(U\text{.}\) Let \(\mathbf{x}_0 \in U\text{.}\) The Hessian matrix of \(f\) at \(\mathbf{x}_0\) is the \(n \times n\) matrix of all second-order partial derivatives:
    \begin{equation*} H_f(\mathbf{x}_0) = \left( \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i \, \partial x_j}(\mathbf{x}_0) \right)_{i,j=1}^{n}. \end{equation*}
    By Theorem 2.1.53, \(H_f(\mathbf{x}_0)\) is symmetric.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.1.55. Hessian Matrix of a Two-Variable Function.

    For \(f(x,y) = x^3 e^{2y}\) (cf.\ Example 2.1.52):
    \begin{equation*} H_f(x,y) = \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} & \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x \, \partial y} \\[6pt] \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y \, \partial x} & \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 6x e^{2y} & 6x^2 e^{2y} \\ 6x^2 e^{2y} & 4x^3 e^{2y} \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    As predicted by Schwarz’ theorem, this matrix is symmetric.

    Exercises 2.1.5 Check Your Understanding

    1. Recognise the Domain.

    Consider the function
    \begin{equation*} f(x,y)=\sqrt{4-x^2-y^2}. \end{equation*}
    Which set is its domain?
    🔗
    • All of \(\R^2\)
    • Incorrect. The expression under the square root must be non-negative.
    • \(\{(x,y)\in\R^2 \mid x^2+y^2 \le 4\}\)
    • Correct. The square root requires \(4-x^2-y^2 \ge 0\text{,}\) which is the closed disk of radius \(2\text{.}\)
    • \(\{(x,y)\in\R^2 \mid x^2+y^2 = 4\}\)
    • Incorrect. Equality gives only the boundary circle, but interior points are allowed too.
    • \(\{(x,y)\in\R^2 \mid x^2+y^2 \ge 4\}\)
    • Incorrect. Outside the circle, the radicand is negative.
    🔗

    2. Use of a Path Test.

    Suppose two different paths approaching the same point \(\mathbf{x}_0\) produce two different function-value limits. What can you conclude?
    🔗
    • The limit at \(\mathbf{x}_0\) does not exist, so the function is not continuous there.
    • Correct. Different path limits immediately rule out the existence of a single multivariate limit.
    • The function is still continuous if the two paths are straight lines.
    • Incorrect. Any two approach paths with different limits are enough to disprove continuity.
    • The function is continuous if one of the two limits equals \(f(\mathbf{x}_0)\text{.}\)
    • Incorrect. Continuity requires all approach paths to give the same limit.
    • No conclusion is possible; one must check every path.
    • Incorrect. A single mismatch already proves that the limit does not exist.
    🔗

    3. Gradient and Directional Derivative.

    Let
    \begin{equation*} f(x,y)=x^2+xy. \end{equation*}
    Compute the gradient at \((1,2)\) and the directional derivative at that point in the direction
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{r}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    First compute the partial derivatives:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=2x+y, \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=x. \end{equation*}
    Hence
    \begin{equation*} \nabla f(1,2) = \begin{pmatrix} 2\cdot 1 + 2\\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4\\ 1 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The directional derivative is the dot product with the unit vector \(\mathbf{r}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{r}}(1,2) = \nabla f(1,2)\cdot \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix}4\\1\end{pmatrix} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix} = \frac{5}{\sqrt{2}}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4. Compute a Hessian Matrix.

    Compute the Hessian matrix of
    \begin{equation*} f(x,y)=x^2+xy+y^2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    The first-order partial derivatives are
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=2x+y, \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=x+2y. \end{equation*}
    Differentiating again gives
    \begin{equation*} f_{xx}=2, \qquad f_{xy}=1, \qquad f_{yx}=1, \qquad f_{yy}=2. \end{equation*}
    Therefore
    \begin{equation*} H_f(x,y)= \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1\\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}
