    Section 3.2 Higher-Order Linear ODEs

    Section 3-1 developed systematic solution methods for first-order ODEs. Many engineering models, however, require higher derivatives: a vibrating mass-spring-damper, the deflection of a loaded beam, and the current in an RLC circuit all yield second- (or higher-) order differential equations.
    🔗
    This section treats the most important subclass: linear ODEs with constant coefficients. We begin by defining the class and identifying its structural features, then develop the characteristic polynomial method for the homogeneous case (Section 3-2.1–3-2.2). For the inhomogeneous case we use the method of undetermined coefficients (Section 3-2.3), whose guessing strategy is completely determined by the form of the right-hand side. The section closes with the harmonic oscillator (Section 3-2.4), the central application that unifies all three sub-topics: the free response uses the homogeneous theory, the forced response uses undetermined coefficients, and the resonance phenomenon illustrates why the distinction between natural frequency and driving frequency matters critically in engineering design.
    🔗

    Subsection 3.2.1 Linear ODEs of Higher Order

    In Section 3-1 we developed systematic solution methods for first-order ODEs. Many physical and engineering phenomena, however, require higher-order equations. A vibrating spring, the bending of a structural beam, or the current in an RLC circuit all give rise naturally to second-order (or higher) differential equations.
    🔗
    This section focuses on the most tractable and practically important subclass: linear ODEs with constant coefficients. For this class a complete algorithmic solution procedure exists, based on factoring a polynomial (the characteristic polynomial) and reading off the solutions from its roots.
    🔗

    Example 3.2.1. Higher-Order ODEs in Engineering.

    Two classical engineering models immediately lead to higher-order linear ODEs with constant coefficients.
    🔗
    1. Harmonic oscillator (mass-spring-damper). A mass \(m\) attached to a spring of stiffness \(k\) and a damper of coefficient \(\alpha\) satisfies
      \begin{equation*} m\, y''(t) + \alpha\, y'(t) + k\, y(t) = F(t), \end{equation*}
      where \(y(t)\) is the displacement from rest and \(F(t)\) is an external force. This is a second-order linear ODE with constant coefficients.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Elastic beam bending. Under a distributed transverse load \(q(x)\text{,}\) the deflection \(w(x)\) of a homogeneous beam with bending stiffness \(EI\) satisfies
      \begin{equation*} E\, I\, w''''(x) = q(x). \end{equation*}
      This is a fourth-order linear ODE.
      🔗
      🔗
    Both examples share the structural feature we will exploit: the unknown function and all its derivatives appear only linearly, and the coefficients are constant.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 3.2.4. Linear ODE of Order \(n\).

    An ODE of order \(n\) is linear if it can be written as
    \begin{equation*} y^{(n)}(t) + a_{n-1}(t)\, y^{(n-1)}(t) + \cdots + a_1(t)\, y'(t) + a_0(t)\, y(t) = f(t), \end{equation*}
    where the coefficient functions \(a_0(t), \ldots, a_{n-1}(t)\) and the right-hand side \(f(t)\) are given. The unknown function \(y(t)\) and its derivatives appear only to the first power and are never multiplied together or nested inside nonlinear functions.
    🔗
    Important special cases:
    • Constant coefficients: all \(a_i(t) = a_i\) are constants.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Homogeneous: \(f(t) = 0\) for all \(t\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Inhomogeneous: \(f(t) \not\equiv 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 3.2.5. What Linearity Rules Out.

    The linearity condition says that \(y\) never appears inside a nonlinear expression. Terms such as \(y^2(t)\text{,}\) \(\sin(y(t))\text{,}\) \(\sqrt{y(t)}\text{,}\) or \(y(t)\, y'(t)\) all violate linearity. By contrast, coefficients like \(\sin(t)\) or \(e^{t}\) are fine, because they depend only on the independent variable \(t\text{,}\) not on the unknown \(y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.2.6. Classifying Higher-Order ODEs.

    Determine for each ODE: is it linear? If so, is it homogeneous? Does it have constant coefficients?
    1. \(\displaystyle 4\, y'''(t) + e^2\, y''(t) + \sin(t)\, y'(t) + \sqrt{y(t)} = 0.\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle y''''(t) + e^2\, y''(t) + \pi\, y'(t) + y(t) = e^{-t}.\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle y'''(t) - \sin(2t)\, y''(t) + \pi\, y'(t) + y(t) = 0.\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Classification.
    1. Not linear: the term \(\sqrt{y(t)}\) is a nonlinear function of \(y\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Linear, not homogeneous, constant coefficients: all derivatives of \(y\) appear linearly; \(f(t) = e^{-t} \neq 0\text{;}\) the coefficients \(1, e^2, \pi, 1\) do not depend on \(t\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Linear, homogeneous, non-constant coefficients: \(f(t) = 0\text{,}\) but the coefficient \(-\sin(2t)\) of \(y''\) depends on \(t\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    The solution methods developed in this section apply to cases like (2): linear, with constant coefficients.
    🔗
    🔗

    \begin{equation*} y''+3y'=0. \end{equation*}

    Subsection 3.2.2 Homogeneous Linear ODEs with Constant Coefficients

    We now develop a complete solution procedure for homogeneous linear ODEs with constant coefficients:
    \begin{equation*} y^{(n)}(t) + a_{n-1}\, y^{(n-1)}(t) + \cdots + a_1\, y'(t) + a_0\, y(t) = 0. \end{equation*}
    The key idea is that exponential trial functions \(y(t) = e^{\lambda t}\) lead naturally to a polynomial in \(\lambda\) — the characteristic polynomial. Its roots determine the entire solution space.
    🔗

    Example 3.2.8. Motivating Case Study.

    Consider the homogeneous third-order ODE
    \begin{equation*} y'''(t) - 3y''(t) + 4y'(t) - 2y(t) = 0. \end{equation*}
    We guess a solution of the form \(y(t) = e^{\lambda t}\text{.}\) Computing derivatives: \(y' = \lambda e^{\lambda t}\text{,}\) \(y'' = \lambda^2 e^{\lambda t}\text{,}\) \(y''' = \lambda^3 e^{\lambda t}\text{.}\) Substituting into the ODE and dividing by \(e^{\lambda t} \neq 0\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \lambda^3 - 3\lambda^2 + 4\lambda - 2 = 0. \end{equation*}
    This algebraic equation — the characteristic polynomial evaluated at \(\lambda\) — must be satisfied. Once we find its roots, we can read off the solutions to the ODE.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 3.2.9. Characteristic Polynomial.

    The characteristic polynomial of the homogeneous linear ODE with constant coefficients
    \begin{equation*} y^{(n)}(t) + a_{n-1}\, y^{(n-1)}(t) + \cdots + a_1\, y'(t) + a_0\, y(t) = 0 \end{equation*}
    is the degree-\(n\) polynomial
    \begin{equation*} P(\lambda) = \lambda^n + a_{n-1}\, \lambda^{n-1} + \cdots + a_1\, \lambda + a_0. \end{equation*}
    The ODE and its characteristic polynomial are obtained from each other by the formal substitution \(y^{(k)}(t) \;\longleftrightarrow\; \lambda^k\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 3.2.10. Counting the Roots.

    By the fundamental theorem of algebra, every polynomial of degree \(n\) over the complex numbers has exactly \(n\) roots counted with multiplicity. Each root of \(P\) contributes one or more linearly independent solutions to the ODE, and together they span the entire solution space (which has dimension \(n\)).
    🔗
    🔗
    Why complex roots produce sine and cosine. Even when the ODE has real coefficients, it is useful to temporarily allow complex-valued trial solutions. If \(\lambda=\alpha+i\beta\) is a complex root, then
    \begin{equation*} e^{\lambda t} = e^{(\alpha+i\beta)t} = e^{\alpha t}\bigl(\cos(\beta t)+i\sin(\beta t)\bigr). \end{equation*}
    The real part \(e^{\alpha t}\cos(\beta t)\) and the imaginary part \(e^{\alpha t}\sin(\beta t)\) are real-valued solutions of the same homogeneous ODE. This is why oscillating sine and cosine terms appear whenever the characteristic polynomial has a complex conjugate pair of roots.
    🔗

    Theorem 3.2.11. Solutions from the Roots of the Characteristic Polynomial.

    Let \(P(\lambda)\) be the characteristic polynomial of a homogeneous linear ODE with constant coefficients, and let \(\lambda_0\) be a root of \(P\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Case 1 — simple real root. If \(\lambda_0 \in \R\) is a simple root, then
    \begin{equation*} y(t) = e^{\lambda_0 t} \end{equation*}
    is a solution.
    🔗
    Case 2 — simple pair of complex conjugate roots. If \(\lambda_0 = \alpha + i\beta\) and \(\overline{\lambda_0} = \alpha - i\beta\) (with \(\alpha, \beta \in \R\text{,}\) \(\beta \neq 0\)) are simple roots, then the two real solutions
    \begin{equation*} y_1(t) = e^{\alpha t}\cos(\beta t), \qquad y_2(t) = e^{\alpha t}\sin(\beta t) \end{equation*}
    are linearly independent solutions.
    🔗
    Case 3 — root of multiplicity \(k \geq 2\text{.}\)
    • Case 3a — real root \(\lambda_0 \in \R\) of multiplicity \(k\text{.}\) The \(k\) linearly independent solutions are
      \begin{equation*} e^{\lambda_0 t},\quad t\, e^{\lambda_0 t},\quad \ldots,\quad t^{k-1} e^{\lambda_0 t}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    • Case 3b — complex root \(\lambda_0 = \alpha + i\beta\) of multiplicity \(k\text{.}\) Conjugacy forces \(\overline{\lambda_0}\) to also be a root of multiplicity \(k\text{.}\) The \(2k\) linearly independent real solutions are
      \begin{align*} e^{\alpha t}\cos(\beta t),\quad \amp e^{\alpha t}\sin(\beta t),\\ t\, e^{\alpha t}\cos(\beta t),\quad \amp t\, e^{\alpha t}\sin(\beta t),\\ \vdots\quad\amp\vdots\\ t^{k-1} e^{\alpha t}\cos(\beta t),\quad \amp t^{k-1} e^{\alpha t}\sin(\beta t). \end{align*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Where the factors \(t, t^2, \ldots\) come from. Write the differential operator on the left-hand side as \(L=P(D)\text{,}\) where \(D\) means differentiation with respect to \(t\text{.}\) If \(\lambda_0\) is a root of multiplicity \(k\text{,}\) then the characteristic polynomial contains the factor \((\lambda-\lambda_0)^k\text{.}\) Correspondingly, the differential operator contains the factor \((D-\lambda_0)^k\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now observe the first step:
    \begin{equation*} (D-\lambda_0)\bigl(t\,e^{\lambda_0 t}\bigr) = e^{\lambda_0 t}. \end{equation*}
    Applying \((D-\lambda_0)\) a second time gives zero. More generally, each factor \(t^j e^{\lambda_0 t}\) is killed after applying \((D-\lambda_0)\) exactly \(j+1\) times. Therefore, if the root has multiplicity \(k\text{,}\) the functions \(e^{\lambda_0 t}, t e^{\lambda_0 t}, \ldots, t^{k-1}e^{\lambda_0 t}\) all solve the ODE. The powers of \(t\) are thus not a trick: they record how many times the same root occurs in the characteristic polynomial.
    🔗
    Principle: adding and scaling homogeneous solutions. Suppose \(y_1(t),\ldots,y_r(t)\) are solutions of the homogeneous linear ODE
    \begin{equation*} y^{(n)} + a_{n-1}y^{(n-1)}+\cdots+a_1y'+a_0y=0. \end{equation*}
    Choose real numbers \(C_1,\ldots,C_r\text{,}\) multiply each solution \(y_j\) by its chosen number \(C_j\text{,}\) and add the results:
    \begin{equation*} y(t)=C_1y_1(t)+\cdots+C_ry_r(t). \end{equation*}
    Then this new function \(y(t)\) is again a solution of the same homogeneous ODE. Such a sum is called a linear combination of the functions \(y_1,\ldots,y_r\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Proof. Let \(L[y]\) denote the left-hand side of the ODE: \(L[y]=y^{(n)}+a_{n-1}y^{(n-1)}+\cdots+a_1y'+a_0y\text{.}\) Differentiation respects scaling and addition, so
    \begin{equation*} L[C_1y_1+\cdots+C_ry_r] = C_1L[y_1]+\cdots+C_rL[y_r]. \end{equation*}
    Since each \(y_j\) solves the homogeneous ODE, each \(L[y_j]\) is zero. Hence
    \begin{equation*} L[y]=C_1\cdot0+\cdots+C_r\cdot0=0, \end{equation*}
    so \(y(t)\) is again a solution.
    🔗

    Theorem 3.2.13. General Solution of Homogeneous Linear ODEs.

    Consider the homogeneous linear ODE with constant coefficients
    \begin{equation*} y^{(n)}(t)+a_{n-1}y^{(n-1)}(t)+\cdots+a_1y'(t)+a_0y(t)=0, \end{equation*}
    and let
    \begin{equation*} P(\lambda)=\lambda^n+a_{n-1}\lambda^{n-1} +\cdots+a_1\lambda+a_0 \end{equation*}
    be its characteristic polynomial. Applying Theorem 3.2.11 to all roots of \(P\text{,}\) counted with multiplicity, yields \(n\) linearly independent solutions \(y_1(t), \ldots, y_n(t)\text{.}\) The general solution is
    \begin{equation*} y(t) = C_1\, y_1(t) + C_2\, y_2(t) + \cdots + C_n\, y_n(t), \qquad C_1, \ldots, C_n \in \R. \end{equation*}
    Initial conditions (e.g. values of \(y(t_0), y'(t_0), \ldots, y^{(n-1)}(t_0)\)) pin down the constants \(C_i\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.2.14. Homogeneous ODE — Double Real Root.

    Find the general solution of \(y''(t) - 2y'(t) + y(t) = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution. The characteristic polynomial is
    \begin{equation*} P(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - 2\lambda + 1 = (\lambda - 1)^2. \end{equation*}
    The root \(\lambda_0 = 1\) has multiplicity \(2\) (Case 3a). The two fundamental solutions are
    \begin{equation*} y_1(t) = e^t, \qquad y_2(t) = t\, e^t. \end{equation*}
    The general solution is
    \begin{equation*} y(t) = C_1\, e^t + C_2\, t\, e^t, \qquad C_1, C_2 \in \R. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.2.15. Homogeneous ODE — Simple Complex Conjugate Roots.

    Find the general solution of \(y''(t) - 2y'(t) + 5y(t) = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution. The characteristic polynomial is \(P(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - 2\lambda + 5\text{.}\) Using the quadratic formula:
    \begin{equation*} \lambda_{1,2} = \frac{2 \pm \sqrt{4 - 20}}{2} = 1 \pm 2i. \end{equation*}
    Thus \(\alpha = 1\text{,}\) \(\beta = 2\) (Case 2). The two fundamental solutions are
    \begin{equation*} y_1(t) = e^t \cos(2t), \qquad y_2(t) = e^t \sin(2t). \end{equation*}
    The general solution is
    \begin{equation*} y(t) = e^t\!\left(C_1 \cos(2t) + C_2 \sin(2t)\right), \qquad C_1, C_2 \in \R. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.2.16. Homogeneous ODE of Order 3 — Mixed Roots.

    Find the general solution of \(y'''(t) - y''(t) + y'(t) - y(t) = 0\) given that \(\lambda = 1\) is a root.
    🔗
    Solution. The characteristic polynomial is \(P(\lambda) = \lambda^3 - \lambda^2 + \lambda - 1\text{.}\) Since \(\lambda = 1\) is a root, we factor out \((\lambda - 1)\) by polynomial division:
    \begin{equation*} P(\lambda) = (\lambda - 1)(\lambda^2 + 1). \end{equation*}
    The roots of \(\lambda^2 + 1 = 0\) are \(\lambda_{2,3} = \pm i\) (i.e. \(\alpha = 0\text{,}\) \(\beta = 1\)). Applying Cases 1 and 2:
    \begin{equation*} y_1(t) = e^t, \quad y_2(t) = \cos(t), \quad y_3(t) = \sin(t). \end{equation*}
    The general solution is
    \begin{equation*} y(t) = C_1\, e^t + C_2 \cos(t) + C_3 \sin(t), \qquad C_1, C_2, C_3 \in \R. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Exercises Check Your Understanding

    🔗

    \begin{equation*} \lambda^2-3\lambda+2=(\lambda-1)(\lambda-2). \end{equation*}
    So the roots are \(1\) and \(2\text{.}\) Therefore the general solution is
    \begin{equation*} y(t)=C_1e^t+C_2e^{2t}, \qquad C_1,C_2\in\R. \end{equation*}

    Subsection 3.2.3 Inhomogeneous Linear ODEs — Method of Undetermined Coefficients

    We now turn to the inhomogeneous linear ODE with constant coefficients:
    \begin{equation*} y^{(n)}(t) + a_{n-1}\, y^{(n-1)}(t) + \cdots + a_1\, y'(t) + a_0\, y(t) = f(t), \qquad f(t) \not\equiv 0. \end{equation*}
    The crucial structural fact is the superposition principle: the general solution is the sum of the general homogeneous solution (which we already know how to find) and any particular solution of the inhomogeneous equation.
    🔗

    Theorem 3.2.17. Superposition Principle.

    Let \(y_p(t)\) be any particular solution of the inhomogeneous ODE
    \begin{equation*} y^{(n)} + a_{n-1} y^{(n-1)} + \cdots + a_0 y = f(t). \end{equation*}
    Let \(y_h(t) = C_1 y_1(t) + \cdots + C_n y_n(t)\) be the general solution of the corresponding homogeneous ODE (i.e.\ with \(f \equiv 0\)). Then the general solution of the inhomogeneous ODE is
    \begin{equation*} y(t) = y_h(t) + y_p(t), \end{equation*}
    and every solution of the inhomogeneous ODE has this form for some choice of \(C_1, \ldots, C_n \in \R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Method of Undetermined Coefficients.

    Building upon the superposition principle (Theorem 3.2.17), we do not need to find all solutions of the inhomogeneous ODE from scratch. Since we already know how to solve the underlying homogeneous ODE, in order to solve an inhomogeneous ODE, it is enough to find one particular solution \(y_p\) satisfying the equation with the given right-hand side \(f(t)\text{;}\) then the full solution is \(y=y_h+y_p\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The method of undetermined coefficients is one possible way to find such a particular solution. Its guiding intuition is that a linear physical system often responds to an external excitation with an answer of the same basic structure: a polynomial input suggests a polynomial response, an exponential input suggests an exponential response, and a sinusoidal input suggests a combination of sine and cosine. We therefore write down a trial function with that structure, leave some coefficients unknown, substitute the trial function into the ODE, and determine the unknown coefficients algebraically.
    🔗
    This leads to the following three-step method for solving an inhomogeneous linear ODE with constant coefficients.
    🔗
    Step 1 — Solve the homogeneous ODE. Find \(y_h(t) = C_1 y_1(t) + \cdots + C_n y_n(t)\) using the characteristic polynomial. The root-to-solution translation is given by Theorem 3.2.11, and the form of the full homogeneous solution is given by Theorem 3.2.13.
    🔗
    Step 2 — Choose a trial function for one particular solution \(y_p(t)\text{.}\) Use the table in Theorem 3.2.18. First write the natural trial function suggested by \(f(t)\text{.}\) Then check whether that same shape already appears among the homogeneous solutions. If it does, multiply the whole trial function by the required power of \(t\text{;}\) this resonance correction is explained in Remark 3.2.20. Finally substitute \(y_p\) into the ODE and solve for the unknown constants in the trial function.
    🔗
    Step 3 — Use superposition to write the general solution. By Theorem 3.2.17, \(y(t) = y_h(t) + y_p(t)\text{.}\) Apply initial conditions to determine \(C_1, \ldots, C_n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 3.2.18. Trial Functions for the Particular Solution.

    Consider an inhomogeneous linear ODE with constant coefficients
    \begin{equation*} y^{(n)}(t)+a_{n-1}y^{(n-1)}(t)+\cdots+a_1y'(t)+a_0y(t)=f(t), \end{equation*}
    and let
    \begin{equation*} P(\lambda)=\lambda^n+a_{n-1}\lambda^{n-1} +\cdots+a_1\lambda+a_0 \end{equation*}
    be the characteristic polynomial of the associated homogeneous ODE. The following table gives the standard trial function for \(y_p(t)\text{.}\) The constants named \(A\) or \(A_j\) are known from the given right-hand side; the constants named \(B\) or \(B_j\) are unknown and will be determined by substitution.
    🔗
    In the table, the “root to check” is the complex number that represents the same exponential or oscillating shape as the forcing term. If that number is a root of \(P\) with multiplicity \(k\text{,}\) multiply the natural trial function by \(t^k\text{.}\) If it is not a root, use \(k=0\text{,}\) so no extra factor of \(t\) is added.
    🔗
    Right-hand side \(f(t)\) Root to check Trial function for \(y_p(t)\)
    Polynomial \(\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{m} A_j t^j\) \(0\) \(t^k\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{m} B_j t^j\)
    \(Ae^{\alpha t}\) \(\alpha\) \(B\,t^k e^{\alpha t}\)
    \(A_1\cos(\beta t)+A_2\sin(\beta t)\) \(i\beta\) and \(-i\beta\) \(t^k\!\left(B_1\cos(\beta t)+B_2\sin(\beta t)\right)\)
    \(e^{\alpha t}\!\left(A_1\cos(\beta t)+A_2\sin(\beta t)\right)\) \(\alpha+i\beta\) and \(\alpha-i\beta\) \(t^k e^{\alpha t}\!\left(B_1\cos(\beta t)+B_2\sin(\beta t)\right)\)
    For a sum of several forcing terms, choose one trial function for each term and add those trial functions together. If the ODE has real coefficients, complex conjugate roots have the same multiplicity, so checking one member of a conjugate pair determines the same value of \(k\) for the other.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 3.2.19. Scope of the Method.

    The method of undetermined coefficients works well when the forcing term \(f(t)\) is built from polynomials, exponentials, sines, cosines, or finite sums and products of these functions. It is not a universal method for arbitrary right-hand sides such as \(\ln t\) or \(\tan t\text{.}\)
    🔗
    That scope restriction is useful to keep in mind: in this section we deliberately stay within the constant-coefficient setting where the forcing term suggests a manageable trial function.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 3.2.20. Why the Resonance Factor \(t^k\text{?}\)

    The case \(k \gt 0\) is called resonance. Algebraically, it means that the natural trial function for \(y_p\) overlaps with a solution of the associated homogeneous ODE. If we try that overlapping trial function, the homogeneous left-hand side either becomes zero or loses exactly the shape we need, so it cannot produce the required forcing term. Multiplying by \(t\) moves the trial function to the next related function shape; if the same characteristic root occurs \(k\) times, we must move \(k\) steps and multiply by \(t^k\text{.}\)
    🔗
    This phenomenon has a physical counterpart: when a driving force matches the natural frequency of an undamped oscillator the amplitude grows without bound (resonance).
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.2.21. Inhomogeneous ODE — Polynomial Right-Hand Side.

    Find the general solution of \(y''(t) - 2y'(t) + y(t) = t^2 + 4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 1 — Homogeneous solution. We found earlier (Example 3.2.14) that the characteristic polynomial is \(P(\lambda) = (\lambda - 1)^2\) and the general homogeneous solution is
    \begin{equation*} y_h(t) = C_1\, e^t + C_2\, t\, e^t. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Step 2 — Particular solution. The right-hand side is a polynomial of degree 2. Check for resonance: \(P(0) = (0-1)^2 = 1 \neq 0\text{,}\) so \(\lambda = 0\) is not a root and \(k = 0\text{.}\) Trial function: \(y_p(t) = B_0 + B_1 t + B_2 t^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Compute the derivatives and substitute into the left-hand side:
    \begin{align*} y_p'(t) \amp= B_1 + 2B_2 t,\\ y_p''(t) \amp= 2B_2. \end{align*}
    \begin{align*} y_p'' - 2y_p' + y_p \amp= 2B_2 - 2(B_1 + 2B_2 t) + (B_0 + B_1 t + B_2 t^2)\\ \amp= (B_0 - 2B_1 + 2B_2) + (-4B_2 + B_1)\, t + B_2\, t^2. \end{align*}
    Equating coefficients with \(f(t) = t^2 + 4 = 4 + 0\cdot t + 1\cdot t^2\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} B_2 \amp= 1,\\ -4B_2 + B_1 \amp= 0 \;\Rightarrow\; B_1 = 4,\\ B_0 - 2B_1 + 2B_2 \amp= 4 \;\Rightarrow\; B_0 = 10. \end{align*}
    Thus \(y_p(t) = 10 + 4t + t^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 3 — General solution.
    \begin{equation*} y(t) = C_1\, e^t + C_2\, t\, e^t + t^2 + 4t + 10, \qquad C_1, C_2 \in \R. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.2.22. Inhomogeneous ODE — Resonance with Exponential Forcing.

    Find the general solution of \(y''(t) + 6y'(t) + 9y(t) = 3e^{-3t}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 1 — Homogeneous solution. \(P(\lambda) = \lambda^2 + 6\lambda + 9 = (\lambda + 3)^2\text{.}\) Root \(\lambda_0 = -3\) has multiplicity 2, so
    \begin{equation*} y_h(t) = C_1\, e^{-3t} + C_2\, t\, e^{-3t}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Step 2 — Particular solution. The right-hand side is \(f(t) = 3\, e^{-3t}\text{,}\) so \(\alpha = -3\text{.}\) Since \(\lambda = -3\) is a root of \(P\) of multiplicity \(k = 2\text{,}\) we use the resonance trial function \(y_p(t) = B\, t^2 e^{-3t}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Compute derivatives (using the product rule twice):
    \begin{align*} y_p' \amp= B(2t - 3t^2) e^{-3t},\\ y_p'' \amp= B(2 - 12t + 9t^2) e^{-3t}. \end{align*}
    Substituting into the ODE:
    \begin{align*} y_p'' + 6y_p' + 9y_p \amp= B e^{-3t}\!\left[(2 - 12t + 9t^2) + 6(2t - 3t^2) + 9t^2\right]\\ \amp= B e^{-3t}\!\left[2 + 0\cdot t + 0\cdot t^2\right] = 2B\, e^{-3t}. \end{align*}
    Setting \(2B = 3\) gives \(B = \tfrac{3}{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 3 — General solution.
    \begin{equation*} y(t) = C_1\, e^{-3t} + C_2\, t\, e^{-3t} + \tfrac{3}{2}\, t^2 e^{-3t}, \qquad C_1, C_2 \in \R. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.2.23. Inhomogeneous ODE — Exponentially Modulated Oscillation.

    Find the general solution of \(y''(t) + 2y'(t) + 5y(t) = e^{-3t}\sin(2t)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 1 — Homogeneous solution. \(P(\lambda) = \lambda^2 + 2\lambda + 5\text{.}\) Roots: \(\lambda_{1,2} = -1 \pm 2i\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} y_h(t) = e^{-t}\!\left(C_1 \cos(2t) + C_2 \sin(2t)\right). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Step 2 — Particular solution. The right-hand side has \(\alpha = -3\text{,}\) \(\beta = 2\text{.}\) The complex frequency \(\alpha + i\beta = -3 + 2i\) is not a root of \(P\) (since the roots are \(-1 \pm 2i\)), so \(k = 0\) and the trial function is
    \begin{equation*} y_p(t) = e^{-3t}\!\left(B_1 \cos(2t) + B_2 \sin(2t)\right). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Let \(u(t) = B_1\cos(2t) + B_2\sin(2t)\text{.}\) After computing \(y_p''+ 2y_p' + 5y_p\) via the product rule and comparing coefficients with \(e^{-3t}\sin(2t)\text{,}\) one obtains the system
    \begin{align*} -3 B_1 + 2 B_2 \amp= 0,\\ -2 B_1 - 3 B_2 \amp= 1. \end{align*}
    Solving: \(B_1 = -\tfrac{2}{13}\text{,}\) \(B_2 = -\tfrac{3}{13}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 3 — General solution.
    \begin{equation*} y(t) = e^{-t}\!\left(C_1\cos 2t + C_2\sin 2t\right) - \tfrac{e^{-3t}}{13}\!\left(2\cos 2t + 3\sin 2t\right), \quad C_1, C_2 \in \R. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Exercises Check Your Understanding

    1. Choose a Trial Function.
    For the ODE
    \begin{equation*} y''-y=\sin t, \end{equation*}
    which trial function is appropriate for a particular solution?
    🔗
    • \(y_p(t)=A\cos t+B\sin t\)
    • Correct. A sine forcing generally requires both sine and cosine terms, because derivatives of sine and cosine produce each other.
    • \(y_p(t)=A\sin t\)
    • Incorrect. This is the common incomplete trial function: after differentiating, cosine terms can appear, so both sine and cosine should be included.
    • \(y_p(t)=At+B\)
    • Incorrect. Polynomial trial functions are used for polynomial forcing terms, not sinusoidal forcing.
    • \(y_p(t)=Ae^t\)
    • Incorrect. Exponential trial functions are used for exponential forcing terms.

    2. Find a Particular Solution in a Resonance Case.
    Find one particular solution of
    \begin{equation*} y''-2y'+y=e^t. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    The homogeneous characteristic polynomial is
    \begin{equation*} (\lambda-1)^2, \end{equation*}
    so \(e^t\) is already a homogeneous solution. We therefore use the resonance trial function
    \begin{equation*} y_p(t)=At^2e^t. \end{equation*}
    Substituting gives
    \begin{equation*} y_p''-2y_p'+y_p=2Ae^t. \end{equation*}
    To match the right-hand side \(e^t\text{,}\) we choose \(2A=1\text{,}\) hence \(A=\tfrac12\text{.}\) Thus one particular solution is
    \begin{equation*} y_p(t)=\tfrac12 t^2 e^t. \end{equation*}

    Subsection 3.2.4 Application: The Harmonic Oscillator

    The techniques developed in this section apply immediately to one of the most important mechanical systems in engineering: the mass-spring-damper system (also called the harmonic oscillator). Beyond its direct engineering relevance — suspension systems, vibration isolation, earthquake engineering — it is the canonical model for any physical system exhibiting oscillatory behaviour near an equilibrium.
    🔗

    Example 3.2.24. The Mass-Spring-Damper System.

    A mass \(m > 0\) is attached to a fixed support by a spring (stiffness \(k > 0\)) and a viscous damper (damping coefficient \(\alpha \geq 0\)). The mass can move vertically; let \(y(t)\) denote its displacement from the natural rest position.
    🔗
    Newton’s second law gives
    \begin{equation*} m\, y''(t) = -k\, y(t) - \alpha\, y'(t) + F(t), \end{equation*}
    which, after dividing by \(m\) and rearranging, becomes
    \begin{equation} y''(t) + \underbrace{\frac{\alpha}{m}}_{2\beta}\, y'(t) + \underbrace{\frac{k}{m}}_{\omega_0^2}\, y(t) = \frac{F(t)}{m}.\tag{3.2.1} \end{equation}
    Physical reasoning constrains the parameters: \(2\beta = \alpha/m \geq 0\) (damping is non-negative) and \(\omega_0^2 = k/m > 0\) (the spring always restores). Here \(\omega_0\) is called the natural frequency.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 3.2.26. Solution Strategy.

    The harmonic oscillator is the concrete place where the abstract theory of this section comes together. First we solve the homogeneous equation (\(F=0\)) to understand the system’s free motion. Then we add a particular solution for the forced case (\(F \neq 0\)), exactly as in Method of Undetermined Coefficients, to describe how an external excitation changes that motion.
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 3.2.27. Case 1a — Free Undamped Oscillation (\(\beta = 0\)).

    With no damping (\(\beta = 0\)), equation (3.2.1) with \(F = 0\) reduces to
    \begin{equation*} y''(t) + \omega_0^2\, y(t) = 0. \end{equation*}
    The characteristic polynomial \(P(\lambda) = \lambda^2 + \omega_0^2\) has the purely imaginary roots \(\lambda_{1,2} = \pm i\omega_0\text{.}\) The general solution is
    \begin{equation*} y(t) = C_1 \cos(\omega_0 t) + C_2 \sin(\omega_0 t). \end{equation*}
    The system oscillates at frequency \(\omega_0\) with constant amplitude — no decay, no growth.
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 3.2.29. Case 1b — Free Damped Oscillation (\(\beta > 0\)).

    With positive damping, the characteristic polynomial \(P(\lambda) = \lambda^2 + 2\beta\lambda + \omega_0^2\) has discriminant \(\Delta = \beta^2 - \omega_0^2\text{.}\) Three qualitatively different behaviours arise.
    🔗
    Case 1b.i — Underdamped (\(\beta^2 \lt \omega_0^2\)). Roots: \(\lambda_{1,2} = -\beta \pm i\omega_d\) where \(\omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \beta^2} > 0\) is the damped natural frequency. General solution:
    \begin{equation*} y(t) = e^{-\beta t}\!\left(C_1\cos(\omega_d t) + C_2\sin(\omega_d t)\right). \end{equation*}
    The system oscillates with exponentially decreasing amplitude.
    🔗
    Case 1b.ii — Critically damped (\(\beta^2 = \omega_0^2\)). Double root \(\lambda_0 = -\beta\text{.}\) General solution:
    \begin{equation*} y(t) = C_1\, e^{-\beta t} + C_2\, t\, e^{-\beta t}. \end{equation*}
    The system returns to rest as fast as possible without oscillating — the aperiodic limit case.
    🔗
    Case 1b.iii — Overdamped (\(\beta^2 > \omega_0^2\)). Two distinct real roots \(\lambda_{1,2} = -\beta \pm \sqrt{\beta^2 - \omega_0^2}\) (both negative, since \(\sqrt{\beta^2 - \omega_0^2} \lt \beta\)). General solution:
    \begin{equation*} y(t) = C_1\, e^{\lambda_1 t} + C_2\, e^{\lambda_2 t}. \end{equation*}
    The system decays to rest without oscillating, but more slowly than in the critically damped case.
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 3.2.31. Case 2 — Forced Oscillation and Resonance.

    Now consider an external sinusoidal force \(F(t) = F_0 \sin(\omega t)\) applied to a damped oscillator (\(\beta \geq 0\)). Using the method of undetermined coefficients with trial function \(y_p(t) = B_1\cos(\omega t) + B_2\sin(\omega t)\) (valid whenever \(\beta > 0\) or \(\omega \neq \omega_0\)), substitution into (3.2.1) gives
    \begin{align*} (\omega_0^2 - \omega^2) B_1 + 2\beta\omega B_2 \amp= 0,\\ -2\beta\omega B_1 + (\omega_0^2 - \omega^2) B_2 \amp= \frac{F_0}{m}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Solving this linear system yields
    \begin{align*} B_1 \amp= -\frac{F_0}{m}\, \frac{2\beta\omega}{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + 4\beta^2\omega^2},\\ B_2 \amp= \frac{F_0}{m}\, \frac{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2}{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + 4\beta^2\omega^2}. \end{align*}
    Hence the particular solution can be rewritten in amplitude-phase form as
    \begin{equation*} y_p(t) = \frac{F_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + 4\beta^2\omega^2}} \sin\!\bigl(\omega t - \varphi(\omega)\bigr), \end{equation*}
    where the phase shift \(\varphi(\omega)\) satisfies \(\tan\varphi = 2\beta\omega/(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The amplitude of the steady-state response is
    \begin{equation*} A(\omega) = \frac{F_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + 4\beta^2\omega^2}}. \end{equation*}
    When \(\beta\) is small and \(\omega \approx \omega_0\text{,}\) the denominator is small and \(A(\omega)\) becomes very large — this is the resonance phenomenon.
    🔗
    In the undamped case \(\beta = 0\) with driving frequency \(\omega = \omega_0\text{,}\) the method of undetermined coefficients requires the resonance trial function \(y_p(t) = t\bigl(B_1\cos(\omega_0 t) + B_2\sin(\omega_0 t)\bigr)\text{,}\) and the amplitude grows linearly without bound.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 3.2.33. How to Read the Frequency-Response Graph.

    For a fixed damping value \(\beta\text{,}\) the graph of \(A(\omega)\) tells us which driving frequencies are dangerous: tall peaks mean that a small periodic force can produce a large steady-state oscillation.
    🔗
    The peak is highest near \(\omega = \omega_0\) when damping is weak. Increasing damping lowers and broadens the peak, which is why damping is an effective engineering tool for suppressing resonance.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 3.2.34. Resonance in Engineering: The Tacoma Narrows Bridge.

    Resonance is not merely a mathematical curiosity. When an external excitation is close to the natural frequency of a structure, even moderate forcing can produce catastrophically large oscillations. A famous historical example is the Tacoma Narrows Bridge (Washington State, USA, 1940), which collapsed just four months after opening due to aerodynamic forces that matched the bridge’s natural frequency. Modern structural engineering avoids resonance by careful tuning of natural frequencies away from expected excitation spectra, and by deliberately introducing damping (e.g.\ tuned mass dampers in skyscrapers).
    🔗
    🔗
